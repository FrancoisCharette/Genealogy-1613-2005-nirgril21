Louis-Guy Lemieux

Le Soleil

His name is, quite simply, Louis-Joseph, Marquis de Montcalm, Seigneur of Saint-Véran, Candiac, Tournemine, Vestric, Saint-Julien and Arpaon, and Baron de Gabriac. He was born at the Château de Candiac, near Nîmes, on February 28, 1712, into a family of the noblesse d'épée (the old military nobility). For his ninth birthday he was made an ensign in the Hainaut regiment.

Even before he came of age, his family bought him the job of captain in the same regiment. Our man was born with a silver sword in his mouth. For want of anyone better, he was put at the head of the French armies in New France. Throughout his short adventure in America, Montcalm harboured a deep dislike of everything Canadian. This gut aversion fixed itself on the person of Governor Vaudreuil, who was born in Canada. From the moment he was appointed, he schemed to get back home as fast as humanly possible. Fate decided otherwise. A stray bullet during the disastrous Battle of the Plains of Abraham ended a career mapped out in advance.

New France is in grave danger in this year of 1756. It is all-out war with England. The fragile French colony needs a thunderbolt of war. The man it sees arriving, with the Chevalier de Lévis, Colonel Bourlamaque and the aide-de-camp Bougainville, at the head of 450 soldiers of the La Sarre and Royal-Roussillon regiments, looks like anything but a great captain.

Physically, Montcalm is short, pudgy and foul-mouthed. His moral qualities don't help. Governor Vaudreuil would soon size him up: vain, nitpicking and pig-headed; he spends more time scheming behind everyone's back than doing his job as a soldier.

When the little French army arrives in the port of Quebec in early May 1756, its commander is not on board. The Marquis de Montcalm has no sea legs. In a hurry to get back on solid ground, he had himself put ashore at Cap Tourmente. He travels overland to Quebec, arriving on May 13. A week later he has himself carried to Montréal in a sedan chair. Those clodhopping Canadiens and their Indian allies are in for a surprise. He is going to show them how war is waged in Europe.

Vaudreuil welcomes Montcalm warmly. The Governor of New France is the superior of the commander of the French troops. Montcalm's commission is very clear on this point. The king wants to avoid the quarrels over precedence that had so hampered French effectiveness in America in the past.

Montcalm's responsibility is limited to discipline, administration and the internal organization of the battalions. He commands only in the field of military operations. He takes his orders from Vaudreuil.

The Fort Edward blunder

At the beginning of 1757, Vaudreuil targets the English positions south of Lake Champlain. Destroying them would block the massive overland attack being planned.

Montcalm gathers 6,200 soldiers, regulars and militiamen, at Carillon, plus 1,800 Indians. Vaudreuil's instructions are to destroy Fort William Henry, at the southern end of Lac Saint-Sacrement (Lake George, N.Y.), then to raze Fort Edward, 16 miles farther south.

After three days of fighting, the commander of Fort William Henry, George Monro, and his 2,500 men surrender. Instead of pressing his advantage and attacking Fort Edward as instructed, Montcalm, who thinks he is still in Europe, plays the grand seigneur and wastes an enormous amount of time granting the honours of war to the defeated garrison. So much so that he forgets about Fort Edward.

Governor Vaudreuil is furious. English morale was at rock bottom. Panic threatened the people of New York. Fort Edward, the gateway to Albany, was only a day's march from William Henry. Montcalm had missed a golden opportunity to bring the English to their knees in this strategic region.

In 1758 the action shifts back to Lake Champlain. There Montcalm wins the victory he would be proudest of.

Major General James Abercromby masses in this area the largest army ever assembled in North America, 15,000 soldiers. Vaudreuil orders Montcalm to fortify Fort Carillon (Ticonderoga) and stop Abercromby's expedition there. Montcalm, as usual, wants to do things his own way. He even thinks of burning Carillon and falling back on Fort Saint-Frédéric (Crown Point, N.Y.). His staff finally persuades him to give battle at Carillon.

Montcalm is lucky. The English army suffers an unexpected delay. Lévis has time to arrive as reinforcement with 400 Canadiens, regulars and militiamen. Montcalm can count on an army of 3,600 men, and he waits for the English on his own ground.

Abercromby makes a series of costly mistakes. He wrongly believes that Montcalm is about to receive 3,000 reinforcements. He attacks the French position without the support of his artillery. Montcalm had left gaping holes in his defences. Too eager to finish things off, Abercromby fails to see these flaws, obvious as they are.

The Battle of Carillon lasts from noon to 7 p.m. on July 8. By the end of the day, badly led, exhausted and decimated, the English troops break and run. They leave behind weapons, ammunition and wounded. The English have lost 1,944 men. The French count 377 dead and wounded.

Montcalm is jubilant. His report to the Minister of War is delirious. He claims to have beaten an army of 30,000 men. He puts the English losses at 5,000. He takes all the credit for the victory. In public he praises the work of the Canadien soldiers but, in secret, he sends a coded message to Paris accusing the Canadiens of cowardice in the face of the enemy. He hints that Vaudreuil did not give him all the help he needed. Thanks to his military genius, he says, he turned a probable defeat into a resounding victory.

The victory at Carillon, together with the fact that Louisbourg was still holding out, spared Canada a full-scale attack in 1758. Historians point out that Carillon only delayed the inevitable in the end. The very next year, the English would be before Quebec.

An impossible man

Montcalm did not lack courage. The five wounds he received on the battlefields of Europe during the War of the Austrian Succession bear witness to that. In Canada, his record is nothing short of catastrophic. If he had not had the good idea of dying on the Plains of Abraham, good historians say, he would have been court-martialled as soon as he got back to France. In his absence, it is poor Vaudreuil who would take the blame.

It is impossible to find a single endearing side to this man. Intrigue serves as his military strategy. Backbiting and slander follow him at every step.

He was not the only Frenchman unable to adapt to the New World. Many young men of good family, dressed up as soldiers, colonial administrators or missionaries, were quietly sent back home. Canada drove more than one of them mad.

From the moment he arrived in Quebec, Montcalm began betraying the governor whose post he coveted, all the while dreaming of a quick return to France.

In his first reports to d'Argenson, he accuses Vaudreuil of caring only about the colonials. In short, he blames him for being born here and for being attached to the country.

After Vaudreuil's victory at Chouaguen (Oswego), he writes to the Minister of the Marine, Machault, to criticize his military tactics. He accuses him of practising « la petite guerre» , that is, guerrilla warfare. Yet it was this way of fighting, learned from the Indians, that had allowed the French of the colony to hold out so long against the English settled to the south and so vastly superior in numbers. Montcalm is blind and deaf to everything that does not belong to his own world.

After the half-victory at Fort William Henry, Montcalm is made a Commander of the Order of Saint-Louis. He uses this honour to ask that, should Vaudreuil die, the post of acting governor general be given to him rather than to the Governor of Montréal, as is customary; as it happens, the Governor of Montréal is Vaudreuil's own brother.

The deserter's wages

By early 1758, Montcalm has already given up hope of saving the French colony of Canada. He writes letters of sickly defeatism to the Minister of the Marine. For him, the die is cast. The English will take Canada without firing a shot. He recommends arranging the capitulation even before the decisive battles. He suggests withdrawing the troops to Louisiana. Given his position, this is close to desertion, all the more so since at the same time he expressly asks to be recalled to France. Yet he has been here only two years.

Then the unbelievable happens. Not only is his request to be recalled turned down, he is promoted. He is made lieutenant general, the second-highest rank in the French military hierarchy. From now on he is Vaudreuil's superior and commands all the military forces in Canada. The stage is set for the great disaster to come.

After the great man's death, his widow, the Marquise de Montcalm, asks for compensation. She claims her husband paid his soldiers out of his own pocket. The government owes her money. An inquiry would prove the opposite. Montcalm had grown rich during his stay in Canada. Historians still wonder today where the 80,000 livres came from that Montcalm managed to save in three years and invest in France.

Sources: the Dictionary of Canadian Biography; La guerre de la Conquête, by Guy Frégault; the Boréal Express; Histoire populaire du Québec, by Jacques Lacoursière.
