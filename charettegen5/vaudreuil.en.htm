VAUDREUIL'S SACRIFICE

Having signed a capitulation to prevent a massacre, 
he is held responsible by the king for the dishonour of the French army

Louis-Guy Lemieux

Le Soleil

 When Louis XV and his ministers learn of the capitulation of New France, they are shaken by the fact that the French army surrendered without being granted the honours of war. The loss of the colony leaves them cold. They care not at all about the fate of the Canadiens, though all of them are of French origin. The Canadiens lick their wounds. The country lies in ruins. The country is bankrupt. There is famine. There is misery. Vaudreuil, the last governor of the French régime, spared his compatriots the worst by sacrificing his career. He must go to France to clear his name.

It is September 18, 1759, the very day Quebec capitulates. Captain John Knox can hardly believe it. Murray sends him with his men to take possession of the town. Seen from outside the walls, the place still looks solid, as indestructible as Cap-aux-Diamants on which it stands. Once through the gates, the sight is heartbreaking.

Not one house has been spared by the shells and fire-pots fired from Pointe-Lévy or the English ships. The lower town has suffered most. It is nothing but a heap of stones and ashes, through which wander hollow-eyed women and children in search of food. Not one house in the upper town is undamaged.

The six-foot-thick walls and vaults have not withstood the cannon. About 180 houses have burned to the ground. The others are hardly better. Both churches are destroyed. Every man able to bear arms has followed the French troops in the rout. Among the civilians left inside the walls are 2,300 women, children and old people. Some 1,200 sick or wounded need care.

The Ursulines and the Hospitallers are exemplary as always. Had it not been for them, the human tragedy would have been even more terrible. They care with the same devotion for Canadiens, French and English, civilians and soldiers alike.

John Knox writes in his journal on September 20: « The havoc is inconceivable... The houses still standing are all more or less pierced by our shells... The least damaged parts of the town are the streets leading to the Saint-Jean, Saint-Louis and Palais gates; even they, however, bear the marks of the almost general destruction» .

The situation in the countryside around Quebec is even more catastrophic, if that is possible.

The whole Côte-de-Beaupré and the Île d'Orléans were sacked before the end of the siege. Houses and farm buildings were burned. The soldiers stole the livestock to feed themselves. Families deprived of their able-bodied men come to Quebec for refuge, making the misery even worse. The only consolation is that the English army spared the churches. They are the only buildings still standing in this landscape laid waste by the war of distant kings of France and England.

On the other side of the river, on the Côte-du-Sud, the country has suffered the same martyrdom. The 19 parishes of the territory stretching to Kamouraska have paid dearly for their resistance to the invader. No village has been spared.  Several must be rebuilt from scratch. All mourn their dead.

The Bishop of Quebec, Mgr de Pontbriand, writes to King Louis XV. He describes the untenable situation. He asks for help most urgently. Governor Vaudreuil, all the responsible military men and the colonial administrators do the same. In vain. France no longer answers, or barely. Four years before the Treaty of Paris, the mother country has already abandoned the French colony in the St. Lawrence valley.

Vaudreuil's honour

The unhappiest prisoner locked up in the Bastille, in Paris, in the spring of 1762, is Pierre de Rigaud, Marquis de Vaudreuil. Aged 64, he keeps reliving in his mind the catastrophic events that led to the fall of France's empire in America. He had been, in the theatre of operations, the leading actor in this drama of several acts. The old governor is humiliated but not beaten.

As soon as he got back to France, on November 28, 1760, a letter from the minister Choiseul was waiting for him at the port of Brest. The king held him personally responsible for the dishonour that had struck the whole French army.

What had shocked Louis XV and his administration most was learning that General Amherst had refused the honours of war to the troops commanded by Lévis at the surrender of Montréal. The victorious English could have put the entire civilian population of the colony to the sword and the king would not have lost his appetite. War is war. But to deny the honours of war to the glorious French army, the most respected in Europe, oh no! never! That is as unacceptable as it is inadmissible. It is worse than losing an empire.

Someone has to be the scapegoat. And there is no question of its being a senior officer of the French army. Governor Vaudreuil was born in Quebec. He is proud of his origins. He will bear the odium of the situation.

As the ultimate humiliation, Vaudreuil's neighbours in the cells are Bigot and his gang of corrupt administrators. He pointedly ignores them. The former intendant will pay for the enormous sums swallowed up in Canada. The last governor of New France will pay for the humiliating military failure.

In his misfortune, Vaudreuil has a clear conscience. He clings to the idea that he did his duty. If he had it to do over, he would act essentially the same way.

At the capitulation of Quebec he had been betrayed by both Montcalm and Ramezay. Concerned first and foremost with the fate of the civilian population, he had lacked firmness from a strictly military point of view. But in human terms he was beyond reproach.

In Montréal, in September 1760, he had faced a cruel dilemma. Three British armies totalling 11,000 men surrounded the last French town still standing in Canada. Montréal was practically defenceless. Everyone, soldiers and civilians alike, urged him to surrender.

It was only on learning that that blockhead Amherst was refusing them a surrender with military dignity that Lévis and Bougainville demanded to make a last sortie with the 2,400 men they had left. At the very least, Lévis asked the governor for the privilege of withdrawing to Île Sainte-Hélène with his men and defying Amherst from there.  That way the senior officers would please Louis XV and save their honour. And they would save their careers.

In his Paris prison, Vaudreuil still breaks into a cold sweat thinking of the hopeless situation he had found himself in. His own career was also at stake.

Giving Lévis a free hand meant the destruction of Montréal and untold suffering inflicted on the Canadien people, abandoned to the fury of the enemy. The British had already shown, in Ireland, in the Scottish Highlands and in Acadia, how ferocious they could be when provoked.

The historian W.J. Eccles points out the strength of character and common sense Governor Vaudreuil showed at that crucial moment. He rejected Lévis's request and signed a capitulation that protected the rights of the Canadiens, their physical safety, their property, their religion and their laws.

In adversity, Vaudreuil had proved himself a great governor, perhaps the greatest in the history of New France.

Events would prove him right. On December 10, 1763, the Châtelet court, which tried the Canada affair, cleared him of all charges. The king had calmed down. He granted him the Grand Cross of the Order of Saint-Louis and an additional pension of 6,000 livres in compensation. The minister Choiseul invited him to dinner, as if to apologize. Even the Chevalier de Lévis testified in his favour.

The Marquis de Vaudreuil would end his life in dignity. Not rich, but upright. That would not, however, bring back his tenderly loved wife, Jeanne-Charlotte Le Verrier, who died of grief during his trial. He would live another 14 years in his house in Paris, on the Rue des Tournelles, in the Latin Quarter.

The fate of the Canadiens

In the eyes of the colony's inhabitants, the fate of Canada was probably decided in the month of June 1760.

A rumour, soon confirmed by the intendant Bigot, causes dismay: the king is no longer redeeming the paper money.  At that point the Canadiens hold bills of exchange worth seven million livres and lettres d'ordonnance (card money) worth 34 million, a colossal sum for the time. It is ruin. The Canadiens already no longer look at the mother country the same way.

From the 18th century on, discontent keeps growing in the colony. Contemptuous and greedy, the nobility and the senior administration demand the same privileges as in France. They grab the juicy contracts at the expense of local merchants. It is hard, if not impossible, for a Canadien to get ahead in business, in the army or even in the clergy. The gap widens between richest and poorest. The common people grow in number and their situation becomes more precarious. The insolent luxury of the elite contemptuously crushes the people of the faubourgs, who live in squalor.

The town of Quebec has an acute problem of begging and vagrancy. The class of craftsmen and tradespeople is sinking into destitution. It is misery. Revolt is brewing.

If, by chance, France had won the Seven Years' War in Europe and the War of the Conquest in America, Canada would have stayed French. And then? Then it is reasonable to think that the Canadiens themselves would have taken it upon themselves to drive the French imperialists out of the territory, just as, a few years later, the neighbours to the south would do with the English from England in the War of Independence.

Apart from career soldiers and the colony's leaders, very few Canadiens go back to France. They were born here. They choose Canada for better or for worse.

Then begins the long battle for survival. A fight that is still going on.

(Sources: the Dictionary of Canadian Biography; Le Boréal Express; Histoire populaire du Québec, by Jacques Lacoursière; Histoire de la ville de Québec, 1608-1871, by John Hare, Marc Lafrance and Thiery Ruddel).
