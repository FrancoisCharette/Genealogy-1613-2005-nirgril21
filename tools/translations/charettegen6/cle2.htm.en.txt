The 2nd revolution: the Constitutional Act of 1791
@@ 1
The Conquest was
        <a href="../charettegen5/cle3.htm#acte" target="_blank">the
        first revolution</a> the Canadiens lived through; their lives were
        turned upside down, often to their advantage. A generation went by, and then came a second revolution,
        that of 1791.
@@ 2
After the American War of Independence, it was the echoes
              of the French Revolution of 1789 that rang out here.
              The British colonial authorities welcomed a revolution
              that came a century after the one England had known in
              1688. Backed by the Catholic Church and M<sup>gr</sup>
              Plessis, the British position changed with the Terror of 1793&nbsp;:
              the beheading of Louis XVI stirred up a powerful counter-revolutionary current in
              the colony.
@@ 3
American independence and the French Revolution
              prompted London, in 1791, to grant its colony a
              Constitution in which monarchical and aristocratic power, embodied
              by the Governor and the Executive and Legislative Councils, was
              tempered by democratic representation.
@@ 4
The
        <b>Loyalists</b>, who had only recently arrived in the
        country, held several important posts in society and wanted
        to lay down the law, either out of excessive patriotism (had they not given up everything
        to stay loyal to England?) or out of revenge (some
        remembered the &quot;<i>petite guerre</i>&quot;, the raids the
        French had carried out with their Native allies in
        New England).&nbsp;<br>
 They sent reports to London calling for
        the assimilation of French Canadians. But the British already had too many problems on their hands to create
        new ones. In 1790, with the threat of war with Spain, England needed the Canadiens to stay loyal, especially if
        the United States sided with Spain.
@@ 5
By 1791 London had had enough of the complaints of the Loyalists, who
        were playing the martyr. True, the Americans had driven them out
        shamefully. But so far the Loyalists' loyalty had cost
        the British Treasury more than 15,000,000 pounds. &quot;<i>They have been
        supplied with seed, livestock and farm implements</i>,
        a Government report in London points out. <i>Every head of
        a family was granted 100 acres, plus 50 acres for each member of his
        family, etc... And they are still complaining</i>, the author of the
        report concludes. <i>There are at most 7,000 of them being more English than the
        English in the territory of Quebec</i>, he scoffs, clearly
        exasperated with the Loyalists, a<i>nd now they would like&nbsp;
        their concessions, from Pointe Beaudet westward, to form a
        district separate from the Province of Quebec
@@ 6
In March,
        <b> William Pitt the Younger</b>, Prime Minister of England,&nbsp; tabled
        a draft Constitution in the Commons that took into account the <i>customs and
        ideas</i> of the Canadiens and the Loyalists. In short: two provinces, Upper
        and Lower Canada; for each, an appointed Legislative
        Council, and an elected Assembly of 50 members for Lower Canada. The
        opposition's reaction was predictable: &quot;<i>Trying to have
        two peoples with different languages, laws and customs is absurd.
        One country, one language. This is meant to separate the English
        inhabitants from the French inhabitants. Would it not be better to work to unite
        the 2 races into a single whole...</i>&quot;&nbsp; Pitt was pragmatic and
        answered them: &quot;<i>It would be extremely desirable for the
        inhabitants of Canada to be united ... but, in the present
        circumstances, dividing the province is probably the best way
        to achieve it. The French subjects will thus be convinced that the
        British government has no intention of imposing English laws
        on them...&nbsp; In time, then, they may adopt our
        laws out of conviction. It is experience that must teach them that
        English laws are the best. But what must be admitted is
        that they must be governed to their satisfaction

THE CONSTITUTIONAL ACT, THE
        CANADIENS'&nbsp;<br>
 SECOND
        REVOLUTION
@@ 8
In
        1791,
        the Constitutional Act thus split Canada in two&nbsp;:
        Upper Canada, mostly
        settled by English-speaking Loyalists, and Lower Canada,
        settled by French Canadians. The territorial
        boundaries of Upper Canada corresponded to today's southern
        Ontario, and those of Lower Canada to today's southern
        Québec.&nbsp;<br>
        <br>
        The Constitutional Act also gave both colonies a
        House of Assembly, which marked the beginnings of democracy in
        Canada. However, these Houses had no real power,
        because the Governor, appointed by the King of Great Britain, had a
        veto over all the House's decisions and also
        the right to dissolve it.&nbsp;<br>
 This sham democracy gave rise
        to movements and debates calling for responsible government,
        which would give legislative power to the people's elected representatives.
@@ 9
In 1791 the Lower Canadian part was given
        a new grid laid over the seigneuries and the
        concessions held in free and common socage: the counties.&nbsp;<br>
        <br>
        The newly established parliamentary system upset the
        social hierarchy by introducing new intermediaries between
        the people, now voters, and the authority of the higher Councils: the
        members of the Assembly. Anyone, even of modest origins, could thus, if
        elected, rise above the seigneur, the nobleman or the curé (parish priest).
@@ 10
Parliamentary life, the growth of printing and the appearance of
              newspapers such as the <i>Gazette de Québec</i>, the <i>Quebec Mercury </i>
              and the <i>Canadien</i> encouraged the birth of public
              opinion and sparked debate. Joseph
              Quesnel captured the era by staging <i> l'Anglomanie</i>
              in 1807. Louis Dulongpré poked fun at it in caricatures.

Parliamentary
              democracy went hand in hand with the rise of a French-speaking
              middle class in the liberal professions, which,
              through professional or political ties or family
              alliances, developed new forms of social life. </font><font size="3" face="Times New Roman"><img border="0" src="images/Amelie.jpg" align="left" hspace="10" vspace="10" width="145" height="168">The bourgeois
              home encouraged pastimes such as watercolour and drawing,

@@ 12
Colleges multiplied. The fur trade, the foundation of
        the economy for two centuries, gave way to the timber industry, which
        ended the essential role of Native people in large-scale
        commerce.&nbsp;<br>
        <br>
        Then came an extraordinary technical advance, the use of
        steam as motive power: it would speed up links between the
        various parts of the country and, with a network of canals, overcome the
        traditional bottlenecks to navigation.

@@ 14
Passed by the Commons, accepted without much
        discussion by the
        House of Lords and given royal assent by the King, the Constitutional Act
        came into force on December 26. In Montréal the event
        went unnoticed: bread was too expensive to hold a banquet.
        In Québec City it was the occasion for memorable celebrations, with many
        toasts raised, from the King to the Duke of Kent, by way of the French Revolution
        and liberty, money and health. Long live the New Constitution....
@@ 15
Everything new is exciting! In 1792, the first election and the
        first election advertising in the newspapers! The one
        <b> Jean-Antone Panet</b> had published in <i> La Gazette de Québec</i> on May 14 is
        particularly refreshing:&quot;<i> Gentlemen and fellow citizens,
        I beg you to grant me your votes and your recommendation at the
        coming general election of your representatives in the Assembly. I have
        the honour to be your most humble and zealous servant.
@@ 16
Once elected, on June 20, Panet
        proved a master
        of publicity. After thanking his voters, he announced that he
        intended to hand out 100 gold louis to the poor. And he kept his word.
        While his colleagues were still congratulating themselves on being
        elected, Panet was already investing in his next election campaign.
@@ 17
Of the 50 members elected, 16 were English-speaking. Of the 6
        members allotted to the city and county of Montréal, 4 were
        English, Joseph Frobisher, James McGill (who had married a French
        Canadian), John Richardson and James Walker, and 2 were Canadiens,
        Jean-Baptiste Durocher and Joseph Papineau. Frobisher, McGill,
        Richardson and Durocher were the Beaver Club, the fur lobby. Walker had been one of the defenders of Fort Saint-Jean in 1775 during
        the American invasion, and Joseph Papineau was a notaire and surveyor;
        it was he who had drawn up the cadastre of most of the concessions around
        Montréal. For those worried that 4 Englishmen were representing
        a Canadien majority, the <i>Gazette de Montréal</i> offered
        reassurance, stating in an article that &quot;<i>these Gentlemen will
        not take into account the ill-founded distinction between English
        and French made by certain prejudiced persons, but will be
        guided only by patriotic motives, for the service of the public good
        and the benefit of the country.
@@ 18
<b> James McGill</b>'s
        first act in the House was to challenge
        Jean-Antoine Panet's candidacy for Speaker of the Assembly
        by putting forward William Grant instead. According to McGill, since a perfect
        knowledge of French and English was an essential
        quality for the Speaker, and Mr. Panet's shortcomings in this
        respect were well known, it stood to reason to choose someone
        perfectly bilingual like Mr. Grant. John
        Richardson's first act was to stress that &quot;<i>dealings between
        the Speaker and the King's representative, the Governor, must be in
        the language of the Empire to which we have the good fortune to belong, whatever
        the language in which the debates in the
        House may have been held</i>&quot;. In his first speech, Joseph Papineau
        agreed that we no doubt had the good fortune to form a branch of
        the British Empire, but &quot;<i>that it cannot be supposed that any
        Canadien should be deprived of his rights because he does not understand
        English.

For the record,
        <b>Joseph Papineau</b>&nbsp; pointed out that, since there were objections
        to Mr. Grant's election, this was an
        obstacle to choosing him as Speaker. The question was then put
        to a vote: Panet was named Speaker by 28 votes to 18, that is,
        all the English members present, joined by 3
        Canadiens. In short, even before the official opening of the
        first parliamentary session of Lower Canada had taken place, the die

After 1793, Lower Canada settled
                      comfortably into parliamentary and
                      partisan quarrels. Everything quickly became a subject of controversy between the
                      French and the British Party. First came the
                      question of the tax and finance laws of 1793 and 1795,
                      which provided for taxing commercial enterprises
                      but not landed property; then came the
                      attempt by J. Mountain, Anglican bishop of Québec, to set up
                      a network of free schools to anglicize and convert the Canadiens as quickly
                      as possible. In 1805 the conflict broke out
                      again, this time over the funding of prisons. On one side were the Canadiens, hostile to a property tax,
                      who suggested taxing commercial
                      activity instead, which they considered a luxury of
                      parasites. On the other was the British Party, which argued that
                      the immense landholdings of the Canadien
                      seigneurs contributed nothing to the general well-being
                      of the colony and should therefore be taxed
                      accordingly.
@@ 21
By giving Upper Canada a distinct identity and
                      a provincial constitution, and by encouraging
                      British settlement there, England took the first
                      step on the road that would finally lead to Canadian
                      Confederation. Even so, many historians consider
                      that the fact that this act did not establish responsible
        government, and that it divided financial
                      powers in a way that favoured the unelected councils,
                      contributed to the political conflicts of the early 19th
                      century.
