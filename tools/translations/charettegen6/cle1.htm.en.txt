Montréal à l'époque de Jacques
@@ title
Montréal in Jacques's day
@@ 1
What was
                the island of Montréal like in Jacques's day?
@@ 2
After the
                Great Peace of 1701, we can look at how
                Montréal developed. Until then, development had been limited to
                Lachine in 1669, Pointe-aux-Trembles in 1678 and
                Sainte-Anne-de-Bellevue in 1685: defensive
                outposts, riverside settlements and trading posts.
@@ 3
In 1702 three
                Décary brothers, Paul, Michel and Louis, began
                clearing the côte Saint-Laurent. The Sulpicians,
                seigneurs of Montréal, granted nineteen concessions on the
                côte Saint-Laurent, which formed the core of the future village.
@@ 4
In the
                following years settlers cleared land and settled on the
                côte Notre-Dame-de-la-Vertu. The côte
                Notre-Dame-de-Liesse was also opened, at the edge of Lachine, and the area
                between Côte-des-Neiges and the Rivière des Prairies slowly filled up.
                So when Jacques Charette was born in 1777, much of the island
                of Montréal was well on its way to being
                cleared.
@@ 5
Since the Conquest of
  1760, British merchants had dominated and controlled almost all of
  Montréal's economy. There were, however, business partnerships
  between the English and the French, especially in the fur trade. The
  French knew the land well and had very good
  relations with those then called &quot;sauvages&quot; (Indigenous peoples). In return, they
  could get access to credit from the British banks, which hardly
  trusted French Canadians. In 1770 James McGill, who had arrived
  in the country three years earlier, went into the fur trade himself
  with French partners. He married a Frenchwoman, Charlotte Des Rivières. Mixed
  marriages were common, which gave the political and religious authorities
  a hard time. Many mixed couples suffered
  discrimination.
@@ 6
Montréal in 1803.
@@ 7
At the time, however, the
  French could still speak their language and practise
  their religion. Home to French, English, Scots, Irish and Jews, the
  city was already cosmopolitan, but also very unequal. British
  officials and merchants, who made up 10% of the
  population, owned 50% of the private land. In 1786 the wealthy John Molson
  opened his brewery. The well-to-do built themselves houses on the
  slopes of Mount Royal. McGill, in fact, had a residence at the foot of the
  mountain, on the very spot where the university that bears
  his name stands today.
@@ 8
Hygiene left
  a lot to be desired in Montréal. At the time people thought water was not
  very good for the skin. They bathed once a week in summer and
  every two weeks in winter.
@@ 9
Notre-Dame
  church was then the largest building in the city. Today the
  Saint-Sulpice seminary, on rue Notre-Dame, bears witness to the architecture of
  the French era. Its bell tower is one of the oldest in America. From 1767
  to 1800, Montréal's population doubled, reaching 9,000 inhabitants. Infrastructure
  was poor and city life was at the mercy of the weather.
@@ 10
Right in the heart
  of the city there were vegetable patches, gardens and cultivated fields. Where
  the Bonaventure expressway runs today, windmills stood.
@@ 11

@@ 12
The open space in front of Notre-Dame
          church served as a public square for meetings and
          gatherings. Town criers made all sorts of announcements there. Keep in
          mind that, at the time, one Montrealer in two could neither
          read nor write.
@@ 13
Farmers also sold their produce
          there, as did the Native people. In winter, public life
          went on in the cafés and taverns, where beer and
          brandy flowed freely.
