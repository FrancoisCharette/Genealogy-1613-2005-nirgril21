La médecine en Nouvelle-France
@@ title
Medicine in New France
@@ 1
I</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">n the 17th and 18th
centuries, illness was closely tied to religion.&nbsp; People believed
it was a punishment or a warning sent to those who disobeyed
God's commandments.&nbsp; Seen this way, prayer was essential to
healing, and the curé (parish priest) played as important a role as the physician or the
surgeon.&nbsp; During epidemics, for example, great processions were organized,
in the firm belief that they would overcome the disease and save the
population from certain death.
@@ 2
I</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">n New France,
suffering was part of everyone's lot, and people sought help only when things
became serious.&nbsp; The physician was the most qualified in the field, since he had to
study for six years before practising.&nbsp; During the French Regime,
some 150 years, five doctors of medicine settled in Canada, and only two had a real
career there.&nbsp; They practised in the towns and served the well-to-do,
the only group able to afford them.&nbsp; There was no medical
school in the country.
@@ 3
S</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">ince physicians
were scarce in the colony, the job of caring for the sick fell mostly
to surgeons.&nbsp;&nbsp;They were relatively numerous, most of them
military men who came with the troops.&nbsp; They were often very young and had
minimal training.&nbsp; Unlike physicians, surgeons practised
not a profession but a trade, which they learned as apprentices to
a master.&nbsp;&nbsp;Most of them were barber-surgeons and
could just as well bleed a patient as trim a beard.
@@ 4
L</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">ike physicians, surgeons
mostly lived in town, and it was very hard for
country people to get their help.&nbsp; Very often, to be treated,
they went to an old woman in the parish who knew a little about natural
remedies and knew how to set fractures.&nbsp; Only in extremely
serious cases did they send for a surgeon.
@@ 5
A</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">t that time, medicine
was based on the Greek and Roman theories of the humours, that is, the
fluids found inside the body.&nbsp; People were convinced that
illness came from an imbalance of the four humours: blood,
phlegm (or pituita), bile and black bile.&nbsp; Depending on which humour won out over
the others, a person would be of a sanguine, phlegmatic, bilious or melancholic temperament.&nbsp;
The treatment and diet required were therefore prescribed according to the
patient's character.
@@ 6
T</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">he physician, and above all the
surgeon, stepped in to drain off excess humours or to
change their nature.&nbsp; To do this, they used bleeding, enemas,
purgatives, emetics, etc.&nbsp; They rarely operated
inside the body, the most complex operations being the removal of a breast or a caesarean section.&nbsp;
Bleeding was one of the most common medical procedures; it
consisted of opening a vein with a lancet to draw off blood.
@@ 7
O</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">ften, people of
modest means preferred to treat themselves with
folk remedies rather than trust
conventional medicine.&nbsp; They relied on a few old recipes.&nbsp; To heal
sores and wounds, for example, they used <i> l'onguent des Paters</i> (the Our Father ointment), which
was made of seven spoonfuls of lard put into a pot while reciting
an Our Father each time.&nbsp; This ointment was said to work real miracles.&nbsp;&nbsp;
People
also believed that drinking the urine of a black cow was enough to cure
tuberculosis, or that eating a slice of bread and butter topped with crushed lice
would cure jaundice.
@@ 8
A</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">mong the various
illnesses affecting the population, from December to March there were
many respiratory problems: sore throats, whooping cough,
fluxion de poitrine (chest congestion), pneumonia, flu and colds.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br>
 There were also plenty
of chilblains, sores, tumours (scrofula), diarrhoea (cour de
ventre), dysentery (flux de sang) and burns, not to mention cases of
gout, rheumatism, kidney ailments and hernia (descente de boyau).&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;<br>
On
top of all these illnesses there were falls, fractures, cuts and
infections that were sometimes fatal.<br>
And of course there were epidemics of typhus, cholera, measles and smallpox
that struck the colony several times.
