I</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">n the 17th century, the population settled mainly around the colony's three
towns, Montréal, Québec and Trois-Rivières.&nbsp; For
years, Iroquois raids slowed population growth, but as soon

T</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">he
St. Lawrence was in fact
the main transportation route, so the seigneuries were granted first
along the river.&nbsp; Not until the 18th century did settlement
begin to spread inland.&nbsp; The space between Montréal and
Québec filled up more and more.&nbsp; In the oldest seigneuries
there were second and even third rangs (rows of farms).&nbsp; When Pehr Kalm, a
Finnish naturalist, visited New France in 1749, he described the
landscape in these terms:

&quot;The country we are passing
is everywhere quite beautiful, and it is a pleasure to see how prettily and how densely
it is settled on both banks of the river; one could almost say
that it forms a continuous village beginning at Montréal and stretching to
Québec, a distance of at least thirty Swedish miles; that is quite a
long village!&nbsp; Almost everywhere, in fact, the farms touch, standing
about three or four arpents apart.&quot;
@@ 6
D</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">uring the 18th
century, many people left the towns for the countryside, so much so
that toward the end of the French Regime, 15% of the population lived in town
and 85% in the country.&nbsp; Before settling, people
made sure they had family or friends nearby, because mutual help was essential to
survival.&nbsp; So it was not unusual to find a cluster of 10 to 50
dwellings in one place; these were called <b> côtes</b> rather than villages.&nbsp;
This closeness was due as much to geography as to family
ties, and among other things it let people stick together in hard
times.
@@ 7
S</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">o people generally settled
in the countryside
near their relatives.&nbsp; A father with a large enough
farm, for example, would leave part of it to his children so they could
build their homes there.&nbsp; The authorities soon sought to control the
subdivision of land so that some people would not end up with properties that were too
small.&nbsp; They even went so far as to tear down houses built
on lots judged too small.

A</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3"> farm covered
about 90 arpents.&nbsp; It had a house, a barn, a stable, and ploughland,
more than a third of it given over to grain.&nbsp;
Another
part, in meadow and fodder, fed the animals.&nbsp; The rest of
the land was in &quot;bois debout&quot; (standing timber), which provided firewood and
building timber.&nbsp; Generally the farmer<span style="mso-spacerun: yes">&nbsp; </span>made sure
to be self-sufficient and went to town only to buy what
he could not make himself: tools, nails, pins, cloth, etc.

T</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">he population of the St. Lawrence
valley grew very fast, from 23,000 people
in 1720 to about 55,000 in 1755 and about 70,000 in 1760.&nbsp;
Natural increase was considerable, since of those 70,000 inhabitants
only an estimated 9,000 to 10,000 were French immigrants, the
rest having been born in the country.
