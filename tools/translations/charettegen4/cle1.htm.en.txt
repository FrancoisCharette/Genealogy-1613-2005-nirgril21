I</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">n the 17th century,
        France had about 636 dialects, which sometimes made communication
        between regions difficult.&nbsp; When travellers
        met, they spoke mostly the Paris patois, called French.&nbsp;
        But each one added a little regional touch, pronounced it in his own way
        and brought in words from his dialect.&nbsp; Sometimes these quirks
        became fashionable and made their way into the Paris
        salons, where grammarians laid down the rules and decided
        what could and could not be said.

M</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">ost of the
        immigrants who landed in New France in the 17th century had their own
        patois, but spoke a practical French.&nbsp; Only a
        third of the population came from Paris; most of the others came from
        Normandy, Aunis, the Perche or Poitou.&nbsp; The Paris basin and the
        northwest strongly influenced the culture that developed
        in the colony and left traces that have lasted through time.

A</b></font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">round 1650, for
        example, immigrants from the Perche settled in New France; they
        came, of course, with their accent and leaned hard on the <i>T</i>.&nbsp;
        They said deboute, lite or nuite.&nbsp; With them, the Paquet family became
        Paquette, Rinfret Rinfrette, the Huots were called Huotte, the Chabots
        Chabotte, and so on.&nbsp; This pronunciation spread, and we
        still hear it today.<br>
        The French that took root in the colony was not the French of the
        grammarians.&nbsp; It was much freer and escaped the new
        standards entirely.&nbsp; When Paris decided, in the 1660s,
        that the verb &quot;japper&quot; should be used for small
        dogs and &quot;aboyer&quot; for big ones, the colony paid no attention
        and went on saying &quot;japper&quot; for every kind of dog.
