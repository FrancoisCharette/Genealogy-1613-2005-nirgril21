@@ 1
The
        Sovereign Council
@@ 2
Louis XIV decided to reign
        as an absolute monarch. The repercussions were&nbsp; major in
        New France, where he set up a system of government
        similar to the one found in the French
        provinces.
@@ 3
People were
        reassured that the king was setting up in Canada a royal government
        answering directly to him, rather than to companies interested
        only in the fur trade and heedless of the settlers'
        needs.&nbsp; From then on, the companies that had
        run the colony no longer had any political power.&nbsp; The new
        system of government no longer served private interests
        but looked after all the needs of the population, and its
        powers reached into every sphere of government:
        administrative, judicial and religious.
@@ 4
The people
        hoped that thanks to these changes a wind of renewal would blow, for
        bad omens had hung over the colony: the financial collapse
        of the fur trade after the Iroquois wars, which had
        destroyed Huronia (near the Great Lakes), an ally of the
        French.&nbsp; There had also been a strong earthquake, and some
        people were &quot;possessed by the devil&quot;.
@@ 5
Before 1663 there
        was the &quot;Council of Quebec&quot;, but it was only
        advisory.&nbsp; The governor was the final authority.&nbsp;
        In 1663 the new structure brought a sharing of power.&nbsp; There
        was now an intendant and a governor, and neither had
        the final say.&nbsp; The intendant was the minister of
        justice, police and finance.&nbsp; The governor could decide
        to make war or give gifts to the Native peoples, but it was
        the intendant who had the money for that war or those gifts.
@@ 6
Louis XIV
        divided in order to rule</i>.&nbsp; There would often be conflict between the
        successive intendants and governors.&nbsp; The Sovereign Council was
        a nest of squabbles and rivalries between people passing through from
        France to boost their careers with a few years in the colony and
        people born here.&nbsp; There were quarrels over precedence among
        governor, intendant and bishop.&nbsp; Each wrote to the king to
        denounce the other...&nbsp;&nbsp; These quarrels
        seem senseless to us today, and yet they are very typical
        of a strongly hierarchical society in which honour and prestige
        were paramount values.
@@ 7
Louis XIV
        encouraged, with letters of nobility, the rise of a small
        colonial nobility.&nbsp; It would be looked down on in France but
        would matter here.
@@ 8
In 1664 the
        &quot;<b><font color="#455287">Coutume de Paris</font></b>&quot; (Custom of Paris) was imposed&nbsp; as civil law,
        that is, the body of civil laws that had existed in the Paris region
        since the 10th century.&nbsp; These laws set out each person's
        personal status, marriage property regimes and the passing on
        of property.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;<br>
        The great feature of the &quot;Coutume de Paris&quot; was
        that on a death it ensured the <b> <font color="#455287"> equal division of property</font></b>.&nbsp; For
        example, if the father died, half his property went to
        his wife, and the other half was shared equally among his children, daughters and
        sons alike.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br>
        This was quite different from the systems
        found elsewhere.&nbsp; In England, for example, the preference was for <b>
        <font color="#455287">
        separate property</font></b>.&nbsp; That is, the will favoured a
        single child, who inherited everything and then looked after his brothers and sisters by
        finding them, or their spouses, work in the family business (e.g. the Molsons).
@@ 9
After the Conquest in 1759, this &quot;Coutume de
        Paris&quot; system meant that the great French-Canadian fortunes would not
        last.&nbsp;&nbsp; British-style fortunes, on the other hand,
        would keep on growing.
@@ 10
The &quot;Coutume de Paris&quot;,&nbsp; applied in New France where
        conditions were different, had unexpected consequences.&nbsp;
        For example, a husband who had to go away for the fur
        trade, or return to France for a while to see to his
        affairs, would delegate to his wife by contract the power to manage their
        assets while he was away.&nbsp; This allowed some women to
        assert themselves in the colony, and so brought a certain emancipation of
        women under the French regime.
@@ 11
Relatively speaking, French-Canadian society resembled
        French society: nobles, bourgeois, merchants, traders,
        professionals, tradesmen, day labourers.&nbsp; Every
        class of society was found there but, unlike France,
        there were neither very rich nor very poor.
@@ 12
In the
        1670s the council became mainly a court of appeal in both civil
        and criminal cases.&nbsp; It was headed by the governor general,
        the intendant and the bishop, along with five to seven councillors.&nbsp; A
        king's attorney general, a clerk and a bailiff completed this
        court.
@@ 13
SEAL
              OF THE SOVEREIGN COUNCIL
@@ 14
The council took a great interest
        in the <b> <font color="#455287"> regulation of public markets, hygiene,
        health and safety</font></b>. Every house, for example, had to
        have latrines; owners were also required to
        clean the front of their homes and sweep their chimneys twice
        a month; it was forbidden to smoke or to throw straw and
        manure into the streets of the town. Merchants could not
        sell their goods door to door before displaying them at the
        market, and the weights and measures they used had to be stamped
        with the king's mark. Most of these regulations were drawn up by
        the intendant and approved by the Council.
@@ 15
The Sovereign
        Council ruled mainly on appeals in <b> <font color="#455287"> civil and
        criminal cases</font></b>, such as petty quarrels between neighbours, thefts and
        murders. Since lawyers were banned in
        New France, plaintiffs and defendants had to plead their
        own cases. Hearings were held every Monday, in private;
        the public could not attend.
@@ 16
A person suspected
        of a crime was arrested and locked up in the royal
        prisons. Then the councillor in charge of the investigation questioned the accused.
        He might well resort to <b> <font color="#455287"> torture</font></b> to
        obtain a confession or the names of accomplices. The accused was then
        put through a terrible ordeal in which &quot;brodequins&quot; (boots) were fitted
        to his legs and tightened hard by the executioner with
        ropes. There were generally two kinds of question: the ordinary
        and the extraordinary. The first differed from the second in the number
        of wooden wedges the torturer drove with a mallet between the
        leg and the wooden board of the boots, and in the intensity
        of the suffering.
@@ 17
Anyone
        found guilty received a <b> <font color="#455287"> punishment</font></b> that varied with the seriousness of the offence,
        from a fine up to the death penalty.<span style="mso-spacerun: yes">&nbsp;
        </span>People convicted of theft were often whipped and branded with
        a red-hot fleur-de-lys on the right shoulder. That way they could
        be identified as <img border="0" src="images/lys1.jpg" align="left" hspace="10" vspace="10" width="54" height="56"> 
        criminals for the rest of their days.
        They could also be sent to the galleys or banished from a
        town or from the colony. Others were simply humiliated in front of
        everyone, exposed in the public square with a placard on their front and
        back, as was the case for Jacques-Phillippe Dalphin, accused of
        theft at the Forges du Saint-Maurice in October 1752 and put in the pillory
        in the market square of Trois-Rivières with a placard that
        read: &quot;Thief of axes belonging to the King&quot;.
@@ 18
Many crimes were punishable
        by death in New France: murder, duelling, counterfeiting,
        burglary, theft by night, theft by a
        servant, rape and arson. Executions
        were always public, to impress the population and
        frighten anyone tempted to break the law. Generally
        the condemned man was hanged, then burned or displayed at a crossroads.
@@ 19
It was, however,
        very hard to find anyone willing to carry out all these
        corporal and afflictive punishments. Although he was housed at the expense of
        the Sovereign Council and well paid, the executioner did not
        have an enviable job. He had to be recruited from the prisons,
        among those condemned to death, who were given the choice between
        becoming executioner or being hanged. Except for Jean Rattier, who held
        the post for 23 years, careers were rather short. In
        1732 the State even had to buy a Black slave to
        serve as executioner in Quebec until 1743.
