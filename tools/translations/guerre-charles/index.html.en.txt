Charles Consigny, my maternal grandfather,
                fought in that war, and came back alive! That is
                surprising when you see even the smallest piece of information about
                this war, called a butchery, a dirty war,
                which killed ten million people and left more than twice as many
                physically or psychologically disabled, the gueules cassées (the disfigured), many
                of whom had to be institutionalized for life.
@@ 4
Charles does not lie
                in a cemetery at Ypres, Arras, Vimy or the Somme.
                Luckily for us, he came home and had
                descendants. But how could he
                survive that hell, bear the suffering and the horror, the
                mud and the vermin, the fear and the anguish? And those
                vain and stupid offensives that mowed men down by the thousands?

By combining the war diaries
                (1), Charles's
                service file supplied by the Archives of Canada (2), various websites, documentaries, books, etc., I
                will try to follow Charles from August 13, 1914, to May 26, 1919,
                and so get a glimpse of his life as a &quot;soldier for King
                and Empire&quot;.
@@ 6
The <b>war diaries</b>
                for each military unit and each medical unit are available on the
                federal Veterans Affairs website. It is the custom in
                every war for a non-commissioned officer to be appointed to keep the
                daily diary of his unit, which is then passed on to the Commanding Officer
                and then to the archives. It contains no personal information, only the day's activities, the
                weather, the number of men, etc... This diary is
                handwritten in English in a small notebook. Canada
                was a Dominion of the British
                Empire.

(2)&nbsp; Charles Consigny's <b>service
                file</b>, obtained from the Archives of Canada,
                includes his medical file, his military postings and
                his banking details, that is, his pay deposits and withdrawals. It
                makes no mention of the battles he took
                part in, or other information of that kind. To find that out,
                you have to cross-check all the data.

                To put things in context, note that when we speak
                of &quot;<u>salvoes</u>&quot; (rounds), it means that each
                gun in the unit fires one shot. So if there are ten guns, a
                salvo means ten shots fired together. Imagine the
                noise on days when hundreds, even thousands, of
                salvoes were fired.

Also,
                when it says that &quot;<u>the unit moves to...</u>&quot;,
                remember that not only the men but also
                the guns, drawn by horses, the wagons, the ammunition,
                etc... moved too. Each unit was a self-contained whole that
                was moved like a pawn. These moves were mostly made
                on foot and at night.
