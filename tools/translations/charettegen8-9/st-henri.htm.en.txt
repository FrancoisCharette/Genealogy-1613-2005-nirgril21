A NEIGHBOURHOOD BURSTING WITH LIFE
@@ 3
Rue Saint-Antoine and its surroundings. Five generations of Charettes
                lived here for seventy years. Between 1870 and
                1940, three were born here and four died here.
@@ 4
Halfway between Lachine and Montréal, <b>Saint-Henri-des-Tanneries</b>
                owes its name to the fur merchants of New France, who opened
                tanneries there to prepare the hides they received from the
                trappers of the Pays-d'en-Haut.
@@ 5
In 1840, Upper and Lower Canada are merged into a
                United Canada with an English-speaking majority. The world
                is changing in this society in the midst of intense demographic
                mixing, above all with the mass arrival of the Irish.
                Steam speeds up shipping and, through the railway, the
                links with the interior of the country. In 1867&nbsp; Canadian
                Confederation gives new momentum to the building of Canada.
@@ 6
The <b>Lachine Canal</b>, dug by hand and
                opened in 1825, allows a large and very diverse manufacturing centre
                to flourish along its banks, right up
                until 1945. Working-class districts grow up around it,
                Saint-Henri among them. Workers' lives are very hard: starvation
                wages for a 6-day, 60-hour week,
                frequent workplace accidents and unemployment in winter, with
                no compensation at all. A quarter of the workers are under sixteen,
                some start at ten, and a third are
                women. After many workers' riots, the Canadian
                government, at the end of the 19th century, imposes better
                sanitary conditions at work and allows workers
                the right to organize.
@@ 7
In wooden houses that are often unhealthy, where <b>
                epidemics</b> of typhus, cholera and smallpox are
                common, the workers, despite their poverty, never forget to
                get together with family and neighbours. They throw parties,
                play sports, sing in the choir. But it is
                the church that is the heart of social life, with Sunday
                mass and the religious feasts.
@@ 8
The <b>Catholic Church</b> becomes a major player in
                society. From the middle of the 19th century, the clergy
                takes control of French-Canadian society
                and meddles in every aspect of its life, even, and above all,
                its morals. It will impose its dictates and its censorship, and hold,
                for a century, a monopoly over associations,
                the press, hospitals,&nbsp; schools and universities. Little by little it
                becomes the voice of French-Canadian nationalism, and it pins
                the people to an agricultural calling, a utopia, given
                the poor state of farming in Québec. The motto seems to be: &quot;<i><font face="Times New Roman">nothing
                must change</font></i>&quot;. The model is &quot;<i><font face="Times New Roman">Maria
                Chapdelaine</font></i>&quot;.<br>
                Many farmers leave their land for the city,
                where factories are opening in which the whole family
                can work without training, most of them being
                illiterate. Others choose the United States: in 1900 the
                population of Québec origin there is estimated at 1,200,000,
                while Québec itself has 1,600,000. To stem this
                emigration, the clergy has new settlement lands opened up,
                such as the Témiscamingue.

THE TWO ALPHONSES IN SAINT-HENRI
@@ 11
Alphonse, father and son, are masonry contractors;
                their houses are of stone and their lives easier than those
                of the factory workers. But they live fully in the heart of Saint-Henri
                and share in its teeming life. Epidemics strike
                the children of the poor and of the better-off alike,
                as we saw with the family of Alphonse Jr.
@@ 12
They are good citizens but, above all, good
                Catholics, because in those days &quot;<i><font face="Times New Roman">outside the Church,
                there is no salvation</font></i>&quot;. Especially for a contractor who
                needs contracts to make a living. The clergy, at the height of its power, has many churches,
                convents, etc. built... all in fine grey cut stone. And that
                is the work of Alphonse father and son. So they were certainly
                on the best of terms with the clergy, probably out of
                religious conviction, since that was the only path taught and socially tolerated, but also out of
                business interest.
