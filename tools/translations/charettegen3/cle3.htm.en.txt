SIX-PAILLES (CIPAILLE)
@@ 34
&quot;T</font><font face="Arial" color="#693E14" size="3">his
              dish is the glory of the housewives of the Gaspé.&nbsp; The
              vagaries of our French-Canadian speech have made it a
              cipare here and a cipâte there.&nbsp; Through an unfortunate similarity
              of pronunciation, it has even become, among our rural people
              who do not understand ten words of English, a &quot;sea pie&quot;,
              although nothing from the sea goes into its
              making.&nbsp; In fact, the name describes this dish made of
              five or six layers of different meats, from the butcher,
              the barnyard and the hunt, separated from one another by as many
              sheets of puff pastry.&nbsp; Right in the middle opens a little
              chimney, kept clear by a paper tube until it is time
              to cook, and removed every hour to flood
              the layers with a gelatinous broth or a rich béchamel
              which, as it cools, binds the whole inside into a succulent
              jelly.&nbsp; That is the traditional, classic theme of the
              six-pailles, which each cook harmonizes and varies to suit her
              taste, her means and her genius.&quot;&nbsp; </font><font face="Arial" color="#693E14" size="2">(Excerpt
              from a Christmas tale by Florandeau)
