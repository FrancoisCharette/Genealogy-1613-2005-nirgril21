MONTREAL,
        AN IMPORTANT TOWN
@@ 2
The
        first Montrealers, fervent zealots.
@@ 3
It was
        around 1636, in the little town of La Flèche in France, that an idea
        began to take root in the mind of a devout man named <font color="#462810"><b> Jérôme</b></font><b><font color="#462810">
        Le Royer, sieur de La Dauversière</font><font color="#616130"> </font></b></font><font face="Arial" size="2">(shown here)</font><font face="Arial" size="3">,
        </font><font face="Times New Roman" size="3">
        a humble tax collector.&nbsp; <br>
        He wished to found a mystical, religious town in the land of America,
        a small community where French and Native people would live in
        harmony in the Christian faith.
@@ 4
The project slowly made its way,
        but only took shape in 1639 with the creation of the &quot;Société
        Notre-Dame de Montréal pour la conversion des Sauvages de la
        Nouvelle-France&quot; (Society of Notre-Dame of Montreal for the conversion of the Savages of New France).&nbsp; Very influential members, such as
        Jean-Jacques Olier, managed to raise enough money to buy
        the island of Montreal from Jean de Lauson in 1640.&nbsp; They then
        hired a leader for their venture, <b><font color="#462810">Paul de
        Chomedey, sieur de Maisonneuve</font></b>, a devout soldier retired from active
        service.&nbsp; Shortly afterwards, <b><font color="#462810">Jeanne
        Mance</font></b> joined them as bursar and nurse, her
        first intention being to set up a hospital.
@@ 5
In 1641 they finally set sail, their heads
        full of the wildest dreams.&nbsp; No one really knew
        the geography or where the island was, and yet everyone was
        certain that faith could overcome every obstacle.&nbsp; The project
        resembled that of the Jesuits, since it consisted of converting and
        settling the &quot; Savages &quot; so as to unite them with the French
        in the Christian religion.&nbsp; Chomedey de Maisonneuve
        officially took possession of Montreal on October 15.&nbsp; The season being
        too far advanced to settle there, he resolved to spend the winter in Quebec,
        where people urged him to settle on Île d'Orléans instead,
        explaining that Montreal was far too exposed to the Iroquois, but
        nothing would change his mind.
@@ 6
In the spring of
        <font color="#462810"><b>1642</b></font>, while the
        members of the Société Notre-Dame met in Paris to
        consecrate the island to the Virgin Mary, Maisonneuve began
        building and fortifying.&nbsp; By the time the first
        snow fell, Montreal already had a palisade, a temporary building
        for Jeanne Mance and the governor, and four little houses where
        the settlers crowded together.
@@ 7
The first winter went well despite
        the fear of a flood.&nbsp; The threat was so serious that
        the Sieur de Maisonneuve promised to plant <b><font color="#462810">a
        cross on Mount Royal</font></b> if they were spared from the waters.&nbsp;
        Then in the spring more comfortable houses were built for the
        settlers.&nbsp; Little by little the town grew, and a few Algonquins
        took refuge inside the palisade.&nbsp; Soon there were
        more of them than at Sillery.&nbsp; This early success, moreover, stirred
        the jealousy of the Jesuits, who accused the Montrealers of
        meddling in diplomatic relations with the Native peoples and
        complicating the fur trade.
@@ 8
It was in the summer of 1643 that the Iroquois
        became aware of the French presence on the island of Montreal and
        began a series of ambushes that spread terror in the
        town.&nbsp; But the Montrealers were brave and did not pack
        up.&nbsp; They had faith and were determined to carry out their
        mission whatever the cost.
@@ 9
In 1645 Jeanne Mance's hospital,
        <b><font color="#462810">the Hôtel-Dieu</font></b>, was built near
        the fort, on high ground.&nbsp; It was of stone and held a
        small chapel.&nbsp; Over the years the establishment
        grew; a well was dug and a larger chapel built,
        which became the parish church.&nbsp; <b><font color="#462810">Marguerite
        Bourgeois</font></b> , for her part, finally managed in 1658, after
        five years in the colony, to open her first school in a stable
        and welcome her first pupils there.
@@ 10
The
        hired men of the Grande Recrue (Great Recruitment)
@@ 11
Because of its location, Montreal
        was especially vulnerable to Iroquois attacks.&nbsp; So the
        population lived in constant fear of an
        attack, and many engagés (indentured men) preferred to go back to France at the end of
        their thirty-six or sixty months rather than settle for good.&nbsp;
        According to the Jesuit Paul Ragueneau, in 1651 the town had about 50
        inhabitants, and of these only 17 were fit to bear
        arms.&nbsp; The situation was critical, and Montreal feared for its survival.
@@ 12
Seeing no other solution, Jeanne
        Mance decided to hand the money she had received for building
        a hospital to the Sieur de Maisonneuve so that he could go <b><font color="#462810"> to find
        recruits in France</font></b>.&nbsp; She revealed to him the identity of her benefactress,
        Madame de Bullion, who had nevertheless asked to remain anonymous.&nbsp; So Maisonneuve visited the lady, who, moved by
        his story, offered him an additional sum.&nbsp; The members of the Société
        de Notre-Dame also contributed, and recruiting
        began.
@@ 13
The losses caused by <b><font color="#462810"> Iroquois
        raids</font></b> had been made up each year by the arrival of five or
        six men, but this time Maisonneuve wanted many more.&nbsp; In that
        year of 1653, he hoped to bring back with him no fewer than 200 soldier-settlers.
@@ 14
His hopes, however, were too high.&nbsp;
        Only 154 volunteers agreed to go with him to the colony.&nbsp;
        On
        the day of departure, only 113 boarded the
        ship, the others having changed their minds along the way.&nbsp; Eight men died
        during the crossing, so that only <b> <font color="#462810"> 105</font></b> landed
        in Montreal.
@@ 15
Among these newcomers were
        surgeons,
        joiners, carpenters, gunsmiths, masons, millers, bakers,
        locksmiths and a coppersmith.&nbsp; There were also
        an edge-tool maker, a hatter, a nail maker, a roofer, a navvy and
        a shoemaker, as well as a clog maker, a tailor and a gardener.
@@ 16
These <b><font color="#462810"> hired men of the Grande Recrue</font></b> were
        a considerable addition, since thanks to them the population of Montreal
        doubled at a stroke.&nbsp; These men came to ply their trades, but
        also undertook to defend the town in case of attack.&nbsp; As soon as they
        arrived, they were given a land grant and a bonus to encourage them
        to stay.&nbsp; It must be said, though, that a lull in Iroquois attacks
        made their settling in much easier.
@@ 17
So the Grande Recrue ensured the survival
        of Montreal.&nbsp; Families came to settle there and clear
        land, and little by little the town took off, but it would not feel
        safe from Iroquois attacks or know peace until the arrival
        of the Carignan-Salières regiment in 1665.
@@ 18
So the little farming colony
        took off, but the plan to include the Native peoples did not have
        the hoped-for success.&nbsp; Although Native people came to stay
        for a while in Montreal, none settled there for good.&nbsp;
        In 1663 the Société de Notre-Dame was dissolved, and a new generation
        took over.&nbsp; The dream of creating, in a remote place, the ideal of
        the original Christian society was now just an old memory.&nbsp;
        From then on, faith gave way to economics.
@@ 19
The
        battle of the Long-Sault
@@ 20
Dollard des Ormeaux: hero or bandit?&nbsp;
        Some historians believe that Dollard saved the colony from certain destruction;&nbsp;
        others see him simply as a vile, deceitful man
        lured by the prospect of gain..&nbsp; But what really happened?&nbsp; Why and how did the
        battle of the Long-Sault take place?
@@ 21
In 1659, under pressure
        from the Iroquois, the Algonquins did not come to the
        annual fair at Ville-Marie, which caused a shortage of furs
        and a lack of funds in the little colony.&nbsp; <b><font color="#462810">Adam Dollard des
        Ormeaux</font></b>,
        a young man well liked by his superiors and fellow citizens and commander of
        the garrison, decided to remedy the situation by setting a trap
        for the Iroquois.&nbsp; He wanted to lie in ambush at the foot of the Long Sault on
        the Ottawa River and surprise those coming back from hunting in the Great
        Lakes with a load of pelts.&nbsp; His plan was to kill them and
        then seize their furs.&nbsp; Governor Chomedey de
        Maisonneuve approved the plan.
@@ 22
So on April 19, <b><font color="#462810">1660</font></b>, Dollard des Ormeaux
        took command of an expedition of 17 men, joined by
        some forty Hurons and six Algonquins.&nbsp; The party travelled
        by night to the Long-Sault.&nbsp; On May 1 it settled into a
        fort abandoned by the Algonquins.&nbsp; The very next day, two canoes
        carrying 15 Iroquois appeared.&nbsp; At once the men took aim and
        fired.&nbsp; Thirteen men were killed, but two managed to escape.
@@ 23
Dollard des Ormeaux's recruits
        stayed put, expecting a few scattered bands
        of Iroquois to turn up.&nbsp; So it was quite a surprise when they saw
        an army of about 200 Iroquois arrive, come to drive
        the French off the island of Montreal for good.&nbsp; Without delay, Dollard's
        group took refuge inside the fort, and shooting broke out on
        both sides.
@@ 24
Even though the French and their allies
        had not foreseen such an encounter, they managed to mount
        an attack and caused many casualties.&nbsp; Some Hurons even
        cut off the head of an enemy chief and set it up as a trophy
        on the palisade, which enraged the Iroquois, who
        hurried off to get help from the 500 Agniers (Mohawks) with
        whom they had planned to meet in the islands of the Richelieu to attack the
        colony.
@@ 25
The Iroquois reinforcements took seven days
        to arrive.&nbsp; Meanwhile, the French could not set foot
        outside the fort without being shot at, and soon they were
        very weak, for water and food were running out.&nbsp; When
        the 500 warriors arrived from the Richelieu, the fight was already lost for
        Dollard and his companions.<br>
        Seeing themselves trapped, the Hurons decided to open talks with
        the enemy, and 24 or 30 of them went over to the other side.&nbsp; Believing that
        the whole party was ready to lay down its arms, a few Iroquois
        approached the fort.&nbsp; But the anxious French opened fire and
        killed the delegates.&nbsp; That was all it took to inflame the hatred of the
        Iroquois, who rushed the palisade.
@@ 26
In a panic, the French took apart two
        pistol barrels to use as grenades. When that
        did not work, they thought of using a keg of powder, but when
        they threw it over the palisade, it hit a branch and
        fell back inside the fort. The resulting explosion was
        catastrophic.&nbsp; The Iroquois took advantage of the situation to move in
        and fire through the loopholes at anything that moved inside
        the fort.&nbsp; When the
        Iroquois got into the fort, they found only nine survivors,
        five Frenchmen and four Hurons.
@@ 27
The obstacles the Iroquois
        met on their way prevented the invasion of
        New France.&nbsp; Dollard des Ormeaux and his companions had thus temporarily
        turned the Iroquois army away from its goal.&nbsp; So their death was not
        in vain.&nbsp; All the more so since these events allowed Radisson and
        Des Groseillers to reach Montreal with a cargo of furs
        valued at 200,000 livres, and a group of Outaouais (Ottawa) to come and trade
        their beaver in Montreal.
@@ 28
But the Iroquois did not stop there.&nbsp;
        They were persistent and, in the autumn of 1660, raised an army of
        600 men, firmly resolved to wipe out New France.&nbsp; On
        the way, the leader of the expedition was killed in an unfortunate
        accident.&nbsp; Taking it as a bad omen, the party broke up.&nbsp;
        In 1661,
        however, the Iroquois came back to spread terror, killing more than 100
        French settlers.&nbsp; It would take the Carignan regiment to bring
        some measure of peace.
@@ 29
Highlights of
        Montreal's early years
@@ 30
The year 1663 marked the end of the first
        period of Montreal's settlement.&nbsp; The Société Notre-Dame,
        no longer able to keep the colony supplied or to pay
        the soldiers and engagés, handed over the seigneury of the island of Montreal
        to the Seminary of Saint-Sulpice in Paris.
@@ 31
The
        <b><font color="#462810">Sulpicians</font></b> governed
        their seigneury.</font>&nbsp; <font face="Times New Roman" size="3">The
        ideals of the mission in the wilderness gave way to the realities of
        daily life, with farming and the fur trade as the main
        economic activities. &nbsp;<br>
        Montreal
        was still only an outpost of French settlement in America,
        but it was already<font color="#462810"> <b>t</b></font><b><font color="#462810">he heart of the fur trade with the Native nations
        of the pays d'en haut</b>.&nbsp;</font></b><font color="#462810">.&nbsp;.&nbsp;</font>  Montreal became the economic
        centre of the colony, and Quebec its administrative centre.
@@ 32
Absolutely all economic life
      revolved around the fur trade.&nbsp; And to carry on this
      trade, you had to go ever farther into the continent, into <b><font color="#462810">the
      Pays d'en Haut</font></b> (Upper Country), that is, all the territories west
      of Montreal that could be reached by
      waterways.
@@ 33
In 1689 the first <a href="cle1.htm" target="_blank">intercolonial
      war</a> began.&nbsp; Furs no longer sold and
      piled up in the warehouses of La Rochelle, where they would
      rot.&nbsp; France wanted to close the western posts, but the
      Iroquois, allies of the English, were still at war, and France had to occupy the
      territory so that it would not fall into English hands.&nbsp; In 1701 the governor of Montreal,
      Callière, negotiated
      the <b><font color="#462810"> Great Peace </font></b> with 33 Native nations,
      including the Iroquois, so that the French
      could sell their furs through the English, who had access to Northern
      Europe.&nbsp; After the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713, the fur trade picked up again
      more strongly than ever.
@@ 34
While everyone was looking westward for Asia and
      its spices, the Spanish reached tropical America and its precious metals.&nbsp;
      The French, English and Dutch, who sailed farther
      north, fell back on &quot;<font color="#462810"><b>hairy gold</b></font>&quot;,
      in other words fur, and in mockery a French outpost set up
      around 1667 on the shores of Lac Saint-Louis was named <font color="#462810"><b>Lachine</b></font> (&quot;China&quot;).&nbsp;
      The historian Faillon wrote: &quot; <i>it was a place more favourable
      than any other for settlers who wished to trade with the savages;
      and at bottom it was less farming than this kind of trade that
      drew most of them</i> &quot;.&nbsp; Clever, daring and
      determined to make their fortune with &quot;pelts&quot;, many
      Canadiens set off on adventures.
@@ 35
Despite the risks of the journey, the
      profits and the feeling of freedom the fur trade brought were so great
      that in 1670 the authorities, fearing the seigneuries would empty out
      in favour of the Pays d'en Haut, tried to crack down by forbidding unmarried men
      to become coureurs des bois (woods runners).&nbsp; A &quot;congé&quot;, or
      permit, was also needed to trade in furs.&nbsp; These permits
      were issued by the civil and religious authorities, and gave rise
      to a great deal of favouritism.&nbsp; So many tried their hand at trading
      without a permit, and had to pay fines if they were
      caught.
