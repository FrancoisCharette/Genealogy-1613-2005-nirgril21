Joseph's beginnings in

@@ 17
After the Conquest of 1759, the <b><a href="../charettegen5/cle3.htm#acte" target="_blank">Quebec Act</a></b> 
        had created a government
        little different from the French regime, since it put power in the
        hands of a governor and a legislative council. The only difference
        was that these rulers were
        mostly English, but it was still government of all by
        a small number of men whose only aim was to get rich. The
        English immigrants were the first to rebel against this regime and
        to&nbsp; demand English laws and the establishment
        of a house of assembly whose members would all be Protestant and
        English.&nbsp;<br>
        <b>French Canadians hesitated</b>. Seeing these new ideas
        championed by the English, they were afraid and feared for their
        nationality in this venture. They preferred the Quebec Act,
        imperfect as it was, to the unknown. But <b>some of them
        were more far-sighted</b> and saw in the proposed
        reforms a springboard for the emancipation of their people.&nbsp;<br>
        They understood that dividing Canada into two provinces, with a
        house of assembly chosen by the people, would give Lower Canada to the
        French Canadians and ensure their supremacy by putting legislative
        power in their hands.&nbsp;<br>
        <b>Joseph Papineau was one of these men</b>. He used all the influence
        he enjoyed to persuade his compatriots to sign the many
        petitions constantly being sent to England from 1783 to 1790. He
        used his gifts as an orator to convince them that the salvation of
        their nationality lay in the very institutions they feared so much.
        &quot;<i>Let His Majesty give us a house of assembly (he said),
        and we will enter it, and there we will be able to defend and preserve our laws
        and set out our wishes and our needs</i>.&quot;&nbsp; The <b><a href="../charettegen6/cle2.htm#2e revolution" target="_blank">Constitutional
        Act</a></b> was signed in 1791, but England did not grant responsible
        government. The governor of Lower Canada divided
        the province into counties, cities and boroughs. <font face="Times New Roman" size="3">For
        the first time in their lives, French Canadians were going to vote.

Louis-Joseph follows

@@ 24
From the very first parliamentary
        session in our history, in 1792, the elected members and the people
        saw the problems of this new political system. The members
        made laws and set taxes in order to improve the lives of
        their fellow citizens, for example to repair roads, open
        schools, etc..., but the governor could divert that money and
        use it for other purposes. He could also end the session at
        any time as soon as the members' work displeased him,
        thereby triggering new elections. To counter this system,
        <b>responsible government had to be won</b>. Louis-Joseph Papineau
        would fight for it throughout his parliamentary life, and even as far as the
        rebellion, though he was above all a moderate man..
@@ 25
<b>Nationalism</b> was born in the Parliament of
        Lower Canada after the political
        crisis of 1810, when Governor Craig wanted the legislative union
        of the two Canadas, and after the members split into <b>political
        parties</b>. Papineau knew how to assert himself and became one of the most
        influential leaders of the time. His nationalism was rather conservative, since
        it was limited to defending the language and institutions of French
        Canada and opposed any form of social revolution.&nbsp;<br>
        The <b> Parti patriote</b> he belonged to split into two groups with similar political
        aims, but differing on
        Québec's social and economic future. The <b>radicals</b> were in
        favour of a social and economic revolution that would abolish the seigneurial
        system and reduce the power of the clergy. For <b>Papineau's
        supporters</b>, on the other hand, these options went against the healthy development
        of a society that had been built and sustained precisely thanks to those
        institutions. Papineau's opposition may also reflect his fear
        of losing the income he drew from the seigneurie he owned.<br>
        <br>
        Papineau was moderate, both in the reforms he sought and in the
        means by which he wanted to achieve them. He advocated legal and
        constitutional means such as refusing to vote the civil list
        and boycotting taxed goods. Although he was opposed to armed
        struggle, in the end it seemed inevitable to him, since many were
        dissatisfied with the results obtained by legal means.
