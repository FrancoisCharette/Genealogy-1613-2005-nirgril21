A</b> short half-hour. The Battle of the Plains of Abraham, on the morning of September 13,
1759, lasted less than an hour, all told. It is a
brief moment in history, heavy with consequences. It heralds the ruin of France's
empire in America. A year later, New France capitulates. In
1763 the Treaty of Paris seals the fate of the French Canadians. The historian
Guy Frégault calls that famous September 13 « the Day of Blunders» . The
two generals, the French Montcalm and the English Wolfe, make
unforgivable mistakes. Neither is up to the job. Quebec surrenders while
victory is still possible.
@@ 5
W</b>hile 4,800 English soldiers form up in battle order on the
Plains, having landed at the Anse au Foulon and climbed the cliff
during the night, General Montcalm is sound asleep at the
Beauport camp.
@@ 6
A</b>round 4 a.m., a Canadien arrives out of breath with the
bad news. The general's secretary, Marcel, wakes his chief.
Montcalm refuses to listen to the messenger: it is impossible for an army
to reach the Plains up the cliff. He bawls out his secretary and goes back
to bed.
@@ 7
A</b>n hour later a second messenger reaches Beauport with a message from
Lieutenant-Colonel de Bernetz, who commands Quebec in Ramezay's absence.
This time there can be no doubt. Montcalm gets up spitting every curse
on earth, as is his habit. Around 6 a.m. he sets off with his
troops toward Quebec. He would have done better to stay in bed.
@@ 8
Montcalm's
battle
@@ 9
T</b>he English armada, 49 warships and 119 troop
transports strong, arrives before Quebec in June. The army sets up on the Île d'Orléans
and at Pointe Lévy.
@@ 10
W</b>olfe commands 8,500 soldiers. Montcalm has more than 15,000
men under him, counting the regular soldiers, the Canadien militiamen and the Indian
allies; he has the advantage of holding a fortified position that the enemy must
take by storm.
@@ 11
T</b>he English have only a few months to take Quebec.
Winter is Montcalm's best ally. All he has to do is hold off the enemy
until the cold season, and the enemy will have to go home
empty-handed. That is reckoning without the pile-up of errors of judgment and
strategic blunders.
@@ 12
F</b>irst major mistake: Pointe Lévy, opposite Quebec, is left
undefended; Admiral Charles Saunders installs 3,000 men and powerful
batteries there.
@@ 13
T</b>he Canadiens had warned Montcalm about this. The French general
and his staff had judged the distance too great for the artillery
to bombard the town. A bad call. For two months, the
English gunners are able to bombard Quebec day and night. They reduce the
capital to ruins.
@@ 14
W</b>hen Vaudreuil authorizes a detachment of volunteers and regular soldiers
to attack Pointe Lévy, it is too late. The position is impregnable.
@@ 15
S</b>econd big mistake: Montcalm is poorly informed about the morale of the
enemy troops and of their leader; he does not know that Wolfe is seriously
ill and that discontent is spreading among his troops.
@@ 16
D</b>isheartened, Wolfe has already decided to lift the siege by September
20 at the latest. The night attack by the Foulon beach is a desperate attempt
to save face.
@@ 17
F</b>inally, Montcalm's most tragic mistake was to give battle
on the Plains, that is, on Wolfe's ground. All he had to do was surround
the enemy and wait for reinforcements from Bougainville, held in reserve
at Cap-Rouge at the head of 3,000 soldiers, the flower of the French army.
@@ 18
W</b>olfe's 4,800 men are frozen with fear on the Plains. They are
surrounded by Canadien militiamen and Indian warriors who, hidden in the
trees and tall grass, pick them off while giving war cries that
chill the blood of the bravest. The English soldiers have only one wish: to
escape the way they came and get back on their boats.
@@ 19
I</b>t is 9 a.m. Montcalm has just come in sight of the Plains. Against
Vaudreuil's advice, he makes the insane decision to fight a fine
European-style battle, with flags, drums and all the trappings. He does not
have the troops for it. He makes the Canadiens line up like
tin soldiers and stupidly expose themselves to enemy fire.
@@ 20
T</b>he historian W. J. Eccles writes in the Dictionary of Canadian Biography:
« <i> Wolfe had put his army in an extremely perilous situation. He was
virtually at Montcalm's mercy. Montcalm chose the one course
of action that ensured his defeat..</i>.» We know the rest.
@@ 21
B</b>eaten and humiliated, Montcalm has to fall back behind the ramparts of Quebec
after his routed army. It is 10 a.m. As he rides his exhausted horse at a walk
through the Porte Saint-Louis, he is hit by a stray bullet. In the
back. He would die the following night. He would be buried in a shell crater under
the Ursulines' chapel.
@@ 22
F</b>our days earlier, the commander-in-chief of the colony's French troops
had already drafted the act of surrender of Quebec. Before he died, and
without telling Governor Vaudreuil, this same Montcalm had time to send
a letter of capitulation to General George Townsend, who had replaced the
mortally wounded Wolfe.

Wolfe's
battle
@@ 24
W</b>olfe is 32 at the time. His health is fragile. He suffers from
chronic rheumatism and gravel (painful kidney stones). During the
month before the final battle he had been bedridden with a serious
attack of dysentery. That may explain his foul temper.
@@ 25
P</b>hysically, he is the opposite of his opponent. Montcalm is short and
round as a pumpkin. Wolfe looks like a tall stalk of asparagus. The two generals
have in common that they were « little better than second-rate commanders»
, in the historian Eccles's words. Both were despised by
the competent officers under them.
@@ 26
D</b>uring the three months of the siege of Quebec, Wolfe keeps
changing strategy. Every initiative he takes ends in failure.
@@ 27
H</b>istorians agree that he was lucky on
the Plains of Abraham, lucky and helped by the monumental mistakes made
by Montcalm. Besides, the idea of attacking by landing upstream from the town
was not his. That strategy came from the officers Townshend, Murray
and Monckton.
@@ 28
F</b>or five weeks no serious battle takes place. The English
troops are depressed. The officers criticize their leader's indecision.
So Wolfe decides on a landing at the Montmorency River on July 31.
It is a stinging failure. The English lose 400 men in the clash.
Several soldiers desert. The rout at Montmorency could have ended
the siege of Quebec.
@@ 29
W</b>olfe takes it very hard. That does not stop him from sending regular troops
and American rangers to lay waste the back country and the Côte-du-Sud. This savage
expedition achieves nothing militarily. It is a black mark on the
record of Wolfe and the whole English army.
@@ 30
G</b>eorge Scott commands a detachment of 1,000 soldiers and rangers. He
would boast of having razed everything over a distance of 52 miles, between Beaumont and
Kamouraska: « <font face="Times New Roman" size="3"><i> We burned 998 good buildings, two sloops, two
schooners, 10 longboats, several flatboats and small craft, and we
took 15 prisoners...
@@ 31
W</b>hat George Scott does not say is that his rangers tortured innocent
settlers to make them tell where they had hidden their gold, and raped
their wives and daughters. It is one of the most gratuitously
cruel episodes of the War of the Conquest.
@@ 32
B</b>y the beginning of September, everything suggests the siege of Quebec will end in
a resounding failure. So Wolfe turns to his three senior officers.
Montcalm must be taken by surprise with a night landing upstream from Quebec, to
cut Bougainville's troops off from those defending Quebec.
@@ 33
T</b>he first idea is to land at Sillery. At the last minute the
attackers switch to the Foulon beach.
@@ 34
M</b>ontcalm had left this sector guarded by about a hundred Canadien
soldiers. They are commanded by one Duchambon de Vergor. Three
quarters of the men have been given leave to go and bring in the hay on their
farms. The few sentries are overpowered. Vergor himself is asleep, probably
knocked out by drink. The English soldiers climb a dry stream bed
in the cliff. Before daybreak the English army holds the Plains.
@@ 35
W</b>olfe has just shut his army into a trap that could be deadly.
Montcalm will be unable to take advantage of it.

@@ 38
G</b>overnor Vaudreuil is considering surrendering Quebec, but not before
exhausting every other possibility.
@@ 39
R</b>amezay, the town's commander, is pushed to give up by a defeatist
local petty bourgeoisie. He calls a council of war. Some voices
call for the fighting to go on, among them that of the officer Jacau de
Fiedmont.
@@ 40
R</b>amezay asks Captain Joannès to raise the white flag. The officer
refuses to obey.
@@ 41
O</b>n September 17 Vaudreuil regains his nerve. He orders Ramezay to
hold firm. The Chevalier de Lévis has just arrived from Montréal. He takes command
of the French troops. He regroups his army in the
Jacques-Cartier valley. He marches on Quebec with Bougainville's
elite troops.
@@ 42
I</b>t is too late. Ramezay cracks. He disregards the governor's orders. He
signs the capitulation. It is September 18, 1759.
@@ 43
O</b>n hearing the news, Lévis cries out in indignation: « <i> It is unheard of
to surrender a place that has been neither attacked nor invested</i>» . Poor
New France!
