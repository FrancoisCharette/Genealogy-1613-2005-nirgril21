THE SURPRISES OF THE MILITARY RÉGIME, 1759-1764
@@ 2
From 1759 to 1764 the Canadiens lived through a
      five-year period of great importance because of the unexpected and
      extraordinary nature of the measures the English applied to the French
      colony, a period that makes this Military Régime a box of
      surprises.
@@ 3
D</b>espite the B<font face="Times New Roman" size="3">ritish
      victory in America, the fighting between France and England is not over
in Europe. Everything is on hold. For nearly five years New France is occupied by
      the British army and governed by martial law.<br>
      <br>
      <b>I</b>mportant precedents are set that would influence the making
      of the Canadian Constitution. This &quot;<b>Canadian model</b>&quot;,
      which developed little by little (trial, error, correction) in the
      colony, was later used by England in other colonies
      as its second Empire expanded after the loss of the United States.
@@ 5
B</b>efore the Conquest, Canada was
      the settled area along the St. Lawrence, with two commercial
      extensions, one to the east, namely the North Shore, Anticosti Island,
      the Magdalen Islands and the Gaspé, and the other, to the west, covering the Great
      Lakes, including Detroit and many trading posts (the pays d'en
      haut, or upper country). The royal government is in Quebec, represented by a
      governor, and there are three administrative regions, Quebec,
      Trois-Rivières and Montréal..
@@ 6
I</b>n
      this framework, more than a century old, a first upheaval occurs in
      the fall of 1759, when the English army occupies, and so cuts off
      from the rest of the country, the Quebec region, that is, from the gulf
      to the Jacques-Cartier River (today the town of Donnacona). The governor, the bishop and the whole administration of the town of Quebec
      take refuge in
      Montréal.
@@ 7
Montréal in 1762
@@ 8
T</b>hen
      comes the first big surprise: Canada
      (the settled St. Lawrence valley) is divided into three separate entities,
      corresponding to the three old administrative regions of Quebec, Trois-Rivières and&nbsp;
      Montréal, but this time the division is
      so strict that these governments are three countries, each under
      the exclusive authority of its governor, separated from one another
      by military borders. Three countries, then, with no longer, as
      before, a national capital uniting the three regions under the same
      authority:&nbsp; each has its own capital. <b> Three
      countries, each sovereign with respect to the other two.</b> If you
      travel from Quebec to Montréal, you show your passport on entering the government of
      Trois-Rivières, at Sainte-Anne-de-la-Pérade, where there is
      a military garrison; again at the next border, at
      Maskinongé, another garrison lets you leave the government of
      Trois-Rivières and finally enter that of Montréal.
@@ 9
T</b>he
      authorities are touchy about this matter of comings and
      goings. No one may move from one country to another without a
      passport from the governor, not even church or
      administrative superiors.
@@ 11
T</b>hree countries, then, marked off by
      borders guarded by soldiers. Three countries that, in addition
      to the same French currency, use an English currency, the pound
      sterling. To complicate matters, the English pound does not
      have the same rate everywhere: in Quebec it follows the
      Halifax rate, whereas in
      Montréal it follows the New York rate. A Canadien of the Military Régime who
      goes to Montréal with English money, and the Conquest
      brought in plenty of it, sees his English pound drop from a value of,
      say, 24 French livres to 15 livres. In
      Trois-Rivières both rates are used...
@@ 12
T</b>he double
      rate for English coin is not the only monetary
      complication. French coinage, which remains in circulation, is
      itself made up of a variety of coins whose
      value has to be worked out in English pounds. There are coins of leather, of
      silver, of gold. Not to mention the English guinea, the Spanish
      piastre, the Portuguese coin... A Canadien's purse is a
      real headache when it comes time to pay the bills.
@@ 13
B</b>ut
      the Conquest brings one big advantage.&nbsp; Since the 17th century the
      colony had regularly run short of coin every fall, since the king's
      ship that was supposed to supply it did not always
      turn up. People had to fall back on paper,
      &quot;<b>card money</b>&quot;. But the King of
      France stopped redeeming the paper money in 1760, and did so
      retroactively for sums owed since 1757.&nbsp; At that point the Canadiens held bills of
      exchange worth seven million livres and lettres
      d'ordonnance (card money) worth 34 million. It was ruin,
      on top of the inflation already raging. French imperialism was beginning to weigh
      very
      heavily on the Canadiens. In fact, France would never repay its
      debts to the Canadiens, only too happy to get off so lightly.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br>
      But the shortage of coin was solved after the
      Conquest. The English came in with
      plenty of coin, available all year round, thanks to the port of New York,
      which was always open.
@@ 15
W</b>hereas
      under the French régime freedom of trade had been very
      limited, the victors granted the inhabitants and merchants
      &quot;<i>all the privileges of trade, with the same favours and conditions
      granted to the subjects of His Britannic Majesty, both in the pays d'en-haut
      and in the interior of the colony</i>&quot;. Later regulations
      would nonetheless limit this freedom. So the Military
      Régime abolished the monopolies that reserved trade
      to one individual or group. Trade became
      open to anyone able to engage in it. It was
      general freedom for entrepreneurs.
@@ 16
P</b>rofits from
      trade were no longer limited to investments with no return:
      lending at interest was no longer condemned as usury.
@@ 18
T</b>o
      the <i>Coutume de Paris</i>, which continued to govern the Canadiens'
      lives in full, were added, on an optional basis, certain rules of the <i>Common
      Law</i>. For example, the age of majority dropped from 25 to 21,
      which was an advantage for young people who wanted to depend
      less long on their parents' authority.
@@ 19
T</b>he militia
      captain of each parish was made judge of first instance. Then courts of hearing were set up, each serving a
      district made up of a number of parishes. So
      ordinary habitants became judges of their peers, though the latter
      could always appeal to a higher court. This system at least
      had the advantage of bringing justice of first instance closer to
      the people.
@@ 20
A REGULAR, VOLUNTARY AND PAID MILITIA
@@ 21
A</b>nother
      surprise of the occupation: voluntary and paid
      military service.&nbsp; Since the militia was created in 1669, every Canadien aged
      16 to 60 had had to serve in wartime; he supplied his own food,
      weapons and clothing and received no pay. Now, in 1763, during
      Indigenous uprisings in the Great Lakes, the English authorities
      wanted to call on the support of the Canadien militia. The
      conditions they offered were completely new: service was
      voluntary, and the militiaman would be armed, fed and clothed by the state and would receive
      pay. These conditions were so out of the ordinary for the
      Canadiens that they hesitated to enlist, so incredulous were they and
      so afraid of a trap. The authorities had to confirm that their offer was
      serious, and ask for the clergy's support, before enlistment got under way.
@@ 22
O</b>n April 27,
      1763, unhappy with the new trade rules introduced by the
            conquerors and wanting to contain European expansion, the chief of the
      Odawa, Pontiac,
            tried to&nbsp;rally the Odawa, the Saulteaux and the
            Fox against the English. In May
            1763 the Indigenous nations organized a series of bloody raids on the
            trading posts of the interior. At Murray's request, the Catholic clergy was forced to
            persuade the Canadiens to volunteer. The Indigenous forces
      were almost completely crushed at Detroit. According to
            the historian Michel Brunet, &quot;<i>Pontiac's war
            inaugurated a new form of collaboration between the English
            conquerors and the church administrators. The latter became
            obliged to defend the British military expeditions to the population
            and to play, in a way, the role
            of real recruiting agents in their service
@@ 24
C</b>ommunications
      improved thanks to a regular postal service. Until then official government mail had been carried by
      officials, and private mail depended on private journeys and the
      goodwill of people travelling from one place to another. In 1763,
      completing the network already set up in the
      Anglo-American colonies, England established an official, regular
      postal service between Quebec and Montréal.
@@ 26
T</b>wo
      English measures, the mile and the yard, were added to the
      French system.&nbsp;In the French system you went straight from the
      foot to the toise (6 feet), so the yard (3 feet) was useful, as
      was the mile, which became an intermediate measure between the arpent and
      the league.

Religion&nbsp;:
@@ 31
T</b>he English
      military authorities agree to allow the Canadiens free exercise of the Catholic
      religion. The only problem is that, since Bishop
      Pontbriand died in 1760, the see remains vacant and
      no one has the authority to appoint a successor. Religious
      authority rests solely with the vicar general of each of the three
      governments, none of whom is in practice subordinate to either of the
      other two. They cannot coordinate their actions. Meanwhile the military authorities are pressing, and questions
      arise. For example, may people be asked to pray for the King of England,
      that heretic? May priests bless a marriage between a
      Catholic and a Protestant?... Isolated in his &quot;country&quot;, each
      vicar general has to find the answer.
@@ 32
T</b>he military
      authorities agree to replace the bishop, but normally
      it is the King of France who chooses him, and the Pope confirms the
      choice with a bull. But now that the King of France no longer has
      any authority over the colony, how could the King of England, regarded as
      a heretic, play that role? All the more so since he regards
      the Pope as an enemy. A terrible dilemma.
@@ 33
E</b>very
      problem has a solution. It is decided to revive an
      old tradition once practised in some European dioceses:
      the election of the bishop by the canons.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br>
      The surprise is that there were two episcopal elections: in September
      1763 the first candidate elected is the Sulpician Montgolfier, a nobleman
      of independent means, superior of the Séminaire de Montréal and Seigneur
      of the Island. Governor Murray does not like this choice. Faced with
      this opposition, Montgolfier prefers to withdraw.&nbsp;&nbsp; </font><img border="0" src="images/mgrBriand.jpg" align="right" hspace="10" vspace="10" width="213" height="268"><br>
      <font face="Times New Roman" size="3">
      In
      September 1764 the canons meet again at the Hôpital-Général
      in Quebec. This time they choose as bishop the vicar general of
      Quebec, <b>Jean-Olivier Briand</b>, a shy, self-effacing but frank
      and level-headed man, a Breton with no personal fortune, a man Murray had several times
      found easy to handle. Briand then obtains
      recognition from Rome, the Pope finally accepting him as a
      &quot;presented bishop&quot;. He goes to France to be
      consecrated bishop on March 16, 1766. Mgr Briand inherits a diocese
      full of problems: churches in disrepair, impoverished communities, shortages
      of priests. What is more, the clergy has lost some of its authority over the
      population. The problem becomes critical when, in 1775, the rebellious
      Americans invade Canada to win the inhabitants over to their
      cause. Briand then issues a pastoral letter urging the population
      to resist the insurgents and support the British, whose
      government had, in his view, been better for the Canadiens than the French
      régime. Although the population in general does not side with the
      Bostonnais (the Americans), Mgr Briand still earns the resentment of some for
      his support of the English régime.

STATUS QUO IN

@@ 35
D</b>uring
      these five years of martial law England let the French Régime
      carry on more or less as it was.&nbsp; The ownership of the movable and
      immovable property of habitants and seigneurs is guaranteed. Despite the division into three
      separate governments, nothing is changed in the boundaries of the
      seigneuries. Nothing changes in the parish system: the
      boundaries are the same, the curé remains the religious head, and the
      civil and military head is still the militia captain, responsible as
      before for passing on the government's military and civil demands to his
      men.
@@ 36
F</b>rench civil law
      continues to apply. Notaires carry on their work
      as before. French coinage and measures
      remain in use. The great fur trade in the
      &quot;pays d'en haut&quot;&nbsp; carries on with the usual use of
      hired engagés, but now without the hindrance of monopolies and permits.
@@ 37
T</b>he
      language of administration is still French. Governors and
      senior officers are bilingual, able to communicate directly
      in French with the Canadiens.
@@ 38
O</b>n Friday, August 10, 1764, at eleven in the morning, on the Place
      d'Armes in Quebec, before the
      troops under arms and a large crowd, the letters
      patent appointing <b> James Murray governor-in-chief of the &quot;<i>Province of
      Quebec</i>&quot;</b> are read.&nbsp;This marks the end of what
      is known as the &quot;Military Régime&quot;, a régime that was, all
      things considered, fairly favourable to the Canadien.
@@ 39
T</b>he country is recovering from the war; famine still
      strikes, inflation reigns, the goods needed to revive trade
      are lacking, and there is deep uncertainty about whether
      France will redeem the card money. Many things are in short supply, but
      everywhere life goes on.
@@ 40
T</b>he occupation troops, cut to a minimum
      after the capitulation of Montréal, and the inhabitants are
      conciliatory toward one another. Apart from a few inevitable clashes
      between former enemies, the governors are convinced the people are
      satisfied with the new government, and the reports sent to London
      say so.
@@ 41
The clergy too is conciliatory; in 1761 people are asked to pray
      in church for the marriage of King George III to Princess
      Charlotte, then for the birth of the Prince of Wales. This attitude of the
      senior clergy during the military régime sets a dangerous
      precedent for its freedom, but perhaps it is the price to pay to
      survive. Until 1768 the English use the chapel of the Hôtel-Dieu
      of Montréal&nbsp; for their services, and do favours for the sisters
      in return. Murray orders a collection among the English officers and soldiers
      to help the poor of Quebec.
