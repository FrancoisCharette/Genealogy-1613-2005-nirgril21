Le
        Soleil
@@ 4
H</b>is name is, quite simply, Louis-Joseph, Marquis de Montcalm, Seigneur of
Saint-Véran, Candiac, Tournemine, Vestric, Saint-Julien and Arpaon,
and Baron de Gabriac. He was born at the Château de Candiac, near Nîmes, on February 28,
1712, into a family of the noblesse d'épée (the old military nobility). For his ninth birthday he was made
an ensign in the Hainaut regiment.
@@ 5
Even before he came of age, his family
bought him the job of captain in the same regiment. Our man was born with a silver
sword in his mouth. For want of anyone better, he was put at the head of the French
armies in New France. Throughout his short adventure in America,
Montcalm harboured a deep dislike of everything Canadian. This
gut aversion fixed itself on the person of Governor Vaudreuil, who was born
in Canada. From the moment he was appointed, he schemed to get back home as fast
as humanly possible. Fate decided otherwise. A stray bullet during the disastrous
Battle of the Plains of Abraham ended a career mapped out in advance.
@@ 6
N</b>ew France is in grave danger in this year of 1756. It is all-out war
with England. The fragile French colony needs a
thunderbolt of war. The man it sees arriving, with the Chevalier de Lévis,
Colonel Bourlamaque and the aide-de-camp Bougainville, at the head of 450
soldiers of the La Sarre and Royal-Roussillon regiments, looks like anything but
a great captain.
@@ 7
P</b>hysically, Montcalm is short, pudgy and foul-mouthed. His moral
qualities don't help. Governor Vaudreuil would soon size him up:
vain, nitpicking and pig-headed; he spends more time scheming
behind everyone's back than doing his job as a soldier.
@@ 8
W</b>hen the little French army arrives in the port of Quebec in early May 1756,
its commander is not on board. The Marquis de Montcalm has no sea legs.
In a hurry to get back on solid ground, he had himself put ashore at Cap
Tourmente. He travels overland to Quebec, arriving on May 13. A week
later he has himself carried to Montréal in a sedan chair. Those clodhopping
Canadiens and their Indian allies are in for a surprise. He is going to
show them how war is waged in Europe.
@@ 9
V</b>audreuil welcomes Montcalm warmly. The Governor of New France
is the superior of the commander of the French troops. Montcalm's
commission is very clear on this point. The king wants to avoid the
quarrels over precedence that had so hampered French effectiveness in America
in the past.
@@ 10
M</b>ontcalm's responsibility is limited to discipline,
administration and the internal organization of the battalions. He commands
only in the field of military operations. He takes his orders from
Vaudreuil.
@@ 11
The Fort Edward
blunder
@@ 12
A</b>t the beginning of 1757, Vaudreuil targets the English positions south of
Lake Champlain. Destroying them would block the massive overland attack being planned.
@@ 13
M</b>ontcalm gathers 6,200 soldiers, regulars and militiamen, at Carillon, plus
1,800 Indians. Vaudreuil's instructions are to destroy Fort William
Henry, at the southern end of Lac Saint-Sacrement (Lake George, N.Y.), then
to raze Fort Edward, 16 miles farther south.
@@ 14
A</b>fter three days of fighting, the commander of Fort William Henry, George
Monro, and his 2,500 men surrender. Instead of pressing his advantage and
attacking Fort Edward as instructed, Montcalm, who thinks he is still
in Europe, plays the grand seigneur and wastes an enormous amount of time granting the honours
of war to the defeated garrison. So much so that he forgets about Fort Edward.
@@ 15
G</b>overnor Vaudreuil is furious. English morale was at rock bottom.
Panic threatened the people of New York. Fort Edward, the gateway to
Albany, was only a day's march from William Henry. Montcalm had
missed a golden opportunity to bring the English to their knees in this strategic
region.
@@ 16
I</b>n 1758 the action shifts back to Lake Champlain. There Montcalm
wins the victory he would be proudest of.
@@ 17
M</b>ajor General James Abercromby masses in this area the largest army
ever assembled in North America, 15,000 soldiers. Vaudreuil orders
Montcalm to fortify Fort Carillon (Ticonderoga) and stop Abercromby's expedition
there. Montcalm, as usual, wants to do things his own way. He
even thinks of burning Carillon and falling back on Fort Saint-Frédéric
(Crown Point, N.Y.). His staff finally persuades him to give battle at
Carillon.
@@ 18
M</b>ontcalm is lucky. The English army suffers an unexpected delay.
Lévis has time to arrive as reinforcement with 400 Canadiens, regulars and
militiamen. Montcalm can count on an army of 3,600 men, and he waits for the
English on his own ground.
@@ 19
A</b>bercromby makes a series of costly mistakes. He wrongly believes that
Montcalm is about to receive 3,000 reinforcements. He attacks the
French position without the support of his artillery. Montcalm had left
gaping holes in his defences. Too eager to finish things off, Abercromby fails to see
these flaws, obvious as they are.
@@ 20
T</b>he Battle of Carillon lasts from noon to 7 p.m. on July 8. By the end of the
day, badly led, exhausted and decimated, the English troops break and run.
They leave behind weapons, ammunition and wounded. The English have lost
1,944 men. The French count 377 dead and wounded.
@@ 21
M</b>ontcalm is jubilant. His report to the Minister of War is delirious. He
claims to have beaten an army of 30,000 men. He puts the English losses
at 5,000. He takes all the credit for the victory. In public he praises the
work of the Canadien soldiers but, in secret, he sends a coded message to
Paris accusing the Canadiens of cowardice in the face of the enemy. He
hints that Vaudreuil did not give him all the help he needed. Thanks
to his military genius, he says, he turned a probable defeat into a resounding
victory.
@@ 22
T</b>he victory at Carillon, together with the fact that Louisbourg was still holding out,
spared Canada a full-scale attack in 1758. Historians point out
that Carillon only delayed the inevitable in the end. The very next
year, the English would be before Quebec.
@@ 23
An impossible
man
@@ 24
M</b>ontcalm did not lack courage. The five wounds he received on the
battlefields of Europe during the War of the Austrian Succession bear witness to that.
In Canada, his record is nothing short of catastrophic. If he had not had the
good idea of dying on the Plains of Abraham, good historians say,
he would have been court-martialled as soon as he got back to
France. In his absence, it is poor Vaudreuil who would take the blame.
@@ 25
I</b>t is impossible to find a single endearing side to this man.
Intrigue serves as his military strategy. Backbiting and slander
follow him at every step.
@@ 26
H</b>e was not the only Frenchman unable to adapt
to the New World. Many young men of good family, dressed up as soldiers,
colonial administrators or missionaries, were quietly sent back
home. Canada drove more than one of them mad.
@@ 27
F</b>rom the moment he arrived in Quebec, Montcalm began betraying the governor whose
post he coveted, all the while dreaming of a quick return to France.
@@ 28
I</b>n his first reports to d'Argenson, he accuses Vaudreuil of caring only
about the colonials. In short, he blames him for being born here and for
being attached to the country.
@@ 29
A</b>fter Vaudreuil's victory at Chouaguen (Oswego), he writes to the Minister of the
Marine, Machault, to criticize his military tactics. He accuses him of
practising « la petite guerre» , that is, guerrilla warfare. Yet it was
this way of fighting, learned from the Indians, that had allowed the French
of the colony to hold out so long against the English settled to the south and
so vastly superior in numbers. Montcalm is blind and deaf to everything that
does not belong to his own world.
@@ 30
A</b>fter the half-victory at Fort William Henry, Montcalm is made a Commander
of the Order of Saint-Louis. He uses this honour to ask that,
should Vaudreuil die, the post of acting governor general
be given to him rather than to the Governor of Montréal, as
is customary; as it happens, the Governor of Montréal is Vaudreuil's own
brother.
@@ 31
The deserter's wages
@@ 32
B</b>y early 1758, Montcalm has already given up hope of saving the French colony
of Canada. He writes letters of sickly defeatism to the Minister of the
Marine. For him, the die is cast. The English will take Canada without
firing a shot. He recommends arranging the capitulation even before the
decisive battles. He suggests withdrawing the troops to Louisiana. Given
his position, this is close to desertion, all the more so since at the same time he
expressly asks to be recalled to France. Yet he has been here only
two years.
@@ 33
T</b>hen the unbelievable happens. Not only is his request to be recalled
turned down, he is promoted. He is made lieutenant general, the
second-highest rank in the French military hierarchy. From now on he is
Vaudreuil's superior and commands all the military forces in
Canada. The stage is set for the great disaster to come.
@@ 34
A</b>fter the great man's death, his widow, the Marquise de Montcalm, asks for
compensation. She claims her husband paid his
soldiers out of his own pocket. The government owes her money. An inquiry would prove the
opposite. Montcalm had grown rich during his stay in Canada. Historians
still wonder today where the 80,000 livres came from that Montcalm
managed to save in three years and invest in France.
