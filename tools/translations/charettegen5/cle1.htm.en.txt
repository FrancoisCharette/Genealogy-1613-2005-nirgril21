In the 1750s the rivalry between France and
        England is felt almost everywhere in the world: in Europe,
        in India, in the West Indies and, of course, in North America, where the differences
        between the French and English colonies are striking. The
        first, though its territory stretches from the Gulf of St. Lawrence
        to the Gulf of Mexico, has a small population of 70,000,
        concentrated in the St. Lawrence valley. The second has
        a population of 1,500,000. Naturally, the size of the armies depends
        on the number of inhabitants.

Although France and England did not officially declare war on each other
until 1756, the conflict that would be called the <i>Seven Years' War</i> began between the
two nations two years earlier in North America, when an influential group
of Anglo-American speculators began to move into the Ohio valley,
rich in fertile land for them and, for France, an important strategic
link between the Great Lakes and Louisiana.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br>
 Forts were built on both
sides of the region. That is how a French officer, Jumonville, was killed
in July 1754 and hostilities began. The French troops,
with the support of Indigenous allies, at first came out on top in the
early clashes; they destroyed forts, &quot;took
        scalps&quot; and carried out raids that more and more exasperated the
        people of New England.

The English army is
made up mainly of regular soldiers, used to fighting in ranks and obeying
orders. The French troops, for their part, are less homogeneous, since
nearly half of them are Canadien militiamen, more used to
guerrilla warfare than to fighting in line. After the first shots, the
Canadien platoons fall back, causing great disorder among the
battalions of regulars. The English, for their part, take two or three volleys
without returning fire.&nbsp;When they finally do fire, the French troops are shaken
and fall back toward the town.

On the day Quebec capitulates, Captain John Knox is sent
to take possession of the town. Seen from outside, the capital of
New France still looks indestructible. But once he goes through
the gates, he can hardly believe his eyes. Not one house has been spared by the English shells
and fire-pots. The lower town is nothing but a heap of smoking
ruins, through which hollow-eyed women and children wander

the town are about 2,300 women, children and old people. They have lost
everything.&nbsp;The men are not there; they are still with what remains of the French
army. The Ursulines care as best they can for the 1,200 sick and
