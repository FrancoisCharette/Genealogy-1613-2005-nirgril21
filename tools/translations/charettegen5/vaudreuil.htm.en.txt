W</b>hen Louis XV and his ministers learn of the capitulation of
New <font face="Times New Roman" size="3">France</font>, they are shaken by the fact that the French army
surrendered without being granted the honours of war. The loss of the colony
leaves them cold. They care not at all about the fate of the Canadiens,
though all of them are of French origin. The Canadiens lick their wounds. The
country lies in ruins. The country is bankrupt. There is famine. There is misery.
<b>
Vaudreuil, the last governor of the French régime</b>, spared his
compatriots the worst by sacrificing his career. He must go to France to clear his
name.
@@ 5
It is September 18, 1759, the very day Quebec capitulates.
Captain John Knox can hardly believe it. Murray sends him with his men to take
possession of the town. Seen from outside the walls, the place still looks
solid, as indestructible as Cap-aux-Diamants on which it stands. Once through the
gates, the sight is heartbreaking.
@@ 6
N</b>ot one house has been spared by the shells and fire-pots fired from
Pointe-Lévy or the English ships. The lower town has suffered
most. It is nothing but a heap of stones and ashes, through which wander
hollow-eyed women and children in search of food.
Not one house in the upper town is undamaged.
@@ 7
T</b>he six-foot-thick walls and vaults have not withstood the
cannon. About 180 houses have burned to the ground. The others are hardly
better. Both churches are destroyed. Every man able to
bear arms has followed the French troops in the rout. Among the
civilians left inside the walls are 2,300 women, children and
old people. Some 1,200 sick or wounded need care.
@@ 8
The Ursulines and the Hospitallers are exemplary as always. Had it
not been for them, the human tragedy would have been even more terrible.
They care with the same devotion for Canadiens, French and
English, civilians and soldiers alike.
@@ 9
J</b>ohn Knox writes in his journal on September 20: « <i> The havoc is
inconceivable... The houses still standing are all more or less pierced
by our shells... The least damaged parts of the town are the streets
leading to the Saint-Jean, Saint-Louis and Palais gates; even they, however, bear
the marks of the almost general destruction
@@ 10
T</b>he situation in the countryside around Quebec is even more
catastrophic, if that is possible.
@@ 11
T</b>he whole Côte-de-Beaupré and the Île d'Orléans were sacked before the
end of the siege. Houses and farm buildings were burned. The
soldiers stole the livestock to feed themselves.&nbsp;Families deprived of their
able-bodied men come to Quebec for refuge, making the misery even
worse. The only consolation is that the English army spared the churches. They are the
only buildings still standing in this landscape laid waste by the war of
distant kings of France and England.
@@ 12
O</b>n the other side of the river, on the Côte-du-Sud, the country has suffered the same
martyrdom. The 19 parishes of the territory stretching to Kamouraska have paid
dearly for their resistance to the invader. No village has been spared.&nbsp;
Several must be rebuilt from scratch. All mourn their dead.
@@ 13
T</b>he Bishop of Quebec, Mgr de Pontbriand, writes to King Louis
        XV. He describes
the untenable situation. He asks for help most urgently. Governor
Vaudreuil, all the responsible military men and the colonial administrators
do the same. In vain. France no longer answers, or barely. Four years before
the Treaty of Paris, the mother country has already abandoned the French colony
in the St. Lawrence valley.
@@ 14
Vaudreuil's
honour
@@ 15
T</b>he unhappiest prisoner locked up in <b> the Bastille</b>, in Paris, in the
spring of 1762, is Pierre de Rigaud, Marquis de Vaudreuil. Aged 64,
he keeps reliving in his mind the catastrophic events that
led to the fall of France's empire in America. He had been, in the theatre
of operations, the leading actor in this drama of several acts. The old
governor is humiliated but not beaten.
@@ 16
A</b>s soon as he got back to France, on November 28, 1760, a letter from the minister
Choiseul was waiting for him at the port of Brest. The king held him personally
responsible for the dishonour that had struck the whole French army.
@@ 17
W</b>hat had shocked Louis XV and his administration most was
learning that General Amherst had refused the honours of war to the
troops commanded by Lévis at the surrender of Montréal. The victorious English
could have put the entire civilian population of the colony to the sword
and the king would not have lost his appetite. War is war.
But to deny the honours of war to the glorious French army, the
most respected in Europe, oh no! never! That is as unacceptable as
it is inadmissible. It is worse than losing an empire.
@@ 18
S</b>omeone has to be the scapegoat. And there is no question of its
being a senior officer of the French army. Governor Vaudreuil was
born in Quebec. He is proud of his origins. He will bear the odium of the
situation.
@@ 19
A</b>s the ultimate humiliation, Vaudreuil's neighbours in the cells are <a href="bigot.htm" target="_blank"> Bigot and his gang</a>
of corrupt administrators. He pointedly ignores them. The former intendant will pay for the enormous sums swallowed up in Canada.
        The
last governor of New France will pay for the humiliating military
failure.
@@ 20
I</b>n his misfortune, Vaudreuil has a clear conscience. He clings to
the idea that he did his duty. If he had it to do over, he would act
essentially the same way.
@@ 21
A</b>t the capitulation of Quebec he had been betrayed by both
Montcalm and Ramezay. Concerned first and foremost with the fate of the
civilian population, he had lacked firmness from a strictly
military point of view. But in human terms he was beyond reproach.
@@ 22
I</b>n Montréal, in September 1760, he had faced a cruel dilemma.
Three British armies totalling 11,000 men surrounded the last
French town still standing in Canada. Montréal was practically
defenceless. Everyone, soldiers and civilians alike, urged him to surrender.
@@ 23
It was only on learning that that blockhead Amherst was refusing them a
surrender with military dignity that Lévis and Bougainville demanded to
make a last sortie with the 2,400 men they had left. At the very least, Lévis
asked the governor for the privilege of withdrawing to Île Sainte-Hélène
with his men and defying Amherst from there.&nbsp; That way the senior officers
would please Louis XV and save their honour. And they would save their careers.
@@ 24
I</b>n his Paris prison, Vaudreuil still breaks into a cold sweat thinking of
the hopeless situation he had found himself in. His own career was
also at stake.
@@ 25
G</b>iving Lévis a free hand meant the destruction of Montréal and
untold suffering inflicted on the Canadien people, abandoned to the fury
of the enemy. The British had already shown, in Ireland, in the
Scottish Highlands and in Acadia, how ferocious they could be
when provoked.
@@ 26
T</b>he historian W.J. Eccles points out the strength of character and common sense
Governor Vaudreuil showed at that crucial moment. He rejected
Lévis's request and signed a capitulation that protected the rights of the Canadiens, their
physical safety, their property, their religion and their laws.
@@ 27
I</b>n adversity, Vaudreuil had proved himself a great governor,
perhaps the greatest in the history of New France.
@@ 28
E</b>vents would prove him right. On December 10, 1763, the Châtelet court,
which tried the Canada affair, cleared him of all charges. The king had calmed down.
He granted him the Grand Cross of the Order of Saint-Louis and an additional
pension of 6,000 livres in compensation. The minister Choiseul invited him to dinner,
as if to apologize. Even the Chevalier de Lévis testified in his favour.
@@ 29
T</b>he Marquis de Vaudreuil would end his life in dignity. Not rich, but upright.
That would not, however, bring back his tenderly loved wife, Jeanne-Charlotte
Le Verrier, who died of grief during his trial. He would live another 14 years in his
house in Paris, on the Rue des Tournelles, in the Latin Quarter.
@@ 30
The fate of the
Canadiens
@@ 31
I</b>n the eyes of the colony's inhabitants, the fate of Canada was probably
decided in the month of June 1760.
@@ 32
A</b> rumour, soon confirmed by the intendant Bigot, causes dismay: the
king is no longer redeeming the paper money.&nbsp; At that point the Canadiens hold
bills of exchange worth seven million livres and lettres
d'ordonnance (card money) worth 34 million, a colossal sum for the time.
It is ruin. The Canadiens already no longer look at the mother country the
same way.
@@ 33
F</b>rom the 18th century on, discontent keeps growing in the
colony. Contemptuous and greedy, the nobility and the senior administration demand
the same privileges as in France. They grab the juicy contracts at the expense
of local merchants. It is hard, if not impossible, for a Canadien to get ahead
in business, in the army or even in the clergy. The gap widens
between richest and poorest. The common people grow in number and
their situation becomes more precarious. The insolent luxury of the elite contemptuously crushes
the people of the faubourgs, who live in squalor.
@@ 34
T</b>he town of Quebec has an acute problem of begging and
vagrancy. The class of craftsmen and tradespeople is sinking into destitution. It is
misery. Revolt is brewing.
@@ 35
I</b>f, by chance, France had won the Seven Years' War in Europe and
the War of the Conquest in America, Canada would have stayed French. And
then? Then it is reasonable to think that the Canadiens themselves
would have taken it upon themselves to drive the French imperialists out of the
territory, just as, a few years later, the neighbours to the
south would do with the English from England in the War of Independence.
@@ 36
A</b>part from career soldiers and the colony's leaders, very
few Canadiens go back to France. They were born here. They choose
Canada for better or for worse.
@@ 37
T</b>hen begins the long battle for survival. A fight that is
still going on.
