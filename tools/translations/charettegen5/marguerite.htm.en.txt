T</b>he nuns who succeeded Marguerite Bourgeois carried on
        her work in education. After years of trying to
        convince the institutional authorities, Sister Sainte-Anne-Marie,
        superior of the Congrégation de Notre-Dame, opened the first École
        supérieure for girls, the first classical college for women in
        Quebec. It was October 8, 1908; located in the mother house on rue
        Sherbrooke, the institution, affiliated with Université Laval, would become
        Collège Marguerite-Bourgeoys in 1926.

        baccalauréat. The first graduate was Marie-Gérin Lajoie. By
        founding Collège Marguerite-Bourgeoys, the Congrégation de Notre-Dame
        held a monopoly&nbsp; on higher education for girls in Quebec
        for many years.
@@ 56
I</b>n 1925 Mother Marie-des-Anges, of the Jésus-Marie religious
        community, founded the Collège Jésus-Marie de Sillery in Quebec City.

        open its doors, the Collège Marie-Anne, ending
        Collège Marguerite-Bourgeoys's monopoly on
        higher education in Montréal. In six years, from 1932 to 1938,
        eight classical colleges for girls were founded in Quebec.

        grounds and shelters in several poor parishes.
        In 1931 she opened her own school of social action. In 1939 she
        helped open the school of social work at the Université
        de Montréal, where she taught the first courses.

T</b>he<b> Chapelle Notre-Dame-de-Bon-Secours</b>, an important site in our historical and
    religious heritage, has been beautifully restored. It holds many
    religious, architectural and artistic treasures. For more than three centuries
    it has been a symbolic gathering place in Montréal's history.
    It was Marguerite-Bourgeoys, with the help of Montréal's first settlers,
    who started the building of the chapel, a place of pilgrimage
    to honour the Virgin Mary.&nbsp;
    <br>
              <b>&nbsp;</b><br>
              <b>T</b>he project dates from 1655, but it was only in 1675 that the
    chapel was finally built, after many difficulties. It would be the first stone
    church in Montréal. It also gave rise to Montréal's first faubourg (suburb),
    and the neighbourhood grew up around it. With the growth
    of the port of Montréal, it became, in the 19th century, the sailors'
    chapel. A symbol of tenacity, it has survived several demolition plans
    in the name of urban development. Pilgrims and visitors from
    all over the world come to pray there and learn about
    this important part of French Canadian history, for many
    figures are connected with it: Pierre du Calvet, Jacques Viger, Louis-Joseph
    Papineau, John Molson and many others...
