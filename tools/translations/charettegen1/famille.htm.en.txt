A FEAST FOR THE EYES
@@ 2
Mathieu brings his wife back to
        Canada.&nbsp; Sébastienne must surely have been dazzled, like every
        newcomer, discovering this new world that seemed beyond all measure
        compared with Europe, built on a human scale.
@@ 3
The mouth of the Seine is five
        kilometres wide; that of the St. Lawrence is about a hundred.&nbsp;
        Even after sailing five hundred kilometres up the <b>river</b>,
        the banks are still thirty kilometres apart, and the water is still
        salty.&nbsp; At Quebec the river is still a kilometre wide, and
        the tide can still be felt.&nbsp; Here, what are called <b>rivières</b> (rivers)
        are in fact great rivers by European standards.&nbsp; What is
        called a <b>lake</b> is in fact an inland sea.&nbsp; There
        are huge <b>islands</b>.&nbsp; The forest is an endless wild
        <b>woodland</b>; you need guides to venture into it.
@@ 4
The <b>distances</b> between
        settlements are greater than Europeans are used
        to.&nbsp; As for farmhouses, so close to one
        another in France, here they are at least a quarter of a
        kilometre apart.&nbsp; The French settler no longer has that human setting in which a
        close-knit community can give a sense of security.

THE S</font>EIGNEURIAL SYSTEM
@@ 6
The seigneurial regime, which
        had existed in France since the Middle Ages, was set up in the colony
        by the Compagnie des Cent-Associés in 1627. It is a <b>system
        of land tenure</b>  <b>that governs how land is held and
        granted </b> in order to encourage settlement of the country.&nbsp; The king named
        someone Seigneur as a reward for some service or on the recommendation
        of the authorities in place.&nbsp; The Seigneur, now a large
        landowner,&nbsp;was encouraged to settle habitants on his domain and
        had to fulfil certain duties toward them in order to keep his seigneury.
        This seigneur had no choice but to develop his seigneury
        to make a profit since a seigneur could not work, except as
        a military officer or in the upper ranks of the colonial
        administration.&nbsp; The regime
        also defined the social and economic relations between
        seigneur and censitaires (tenants).
@@ 7
But <b> before land can be
        granted, it must be divided up</b>, and the St. Lawrence River serves
        as a natural dividing line. Since it is the only route
        of communication from one end of the country to the other, everyone
        wants access to it. So the colony's territory is divided into <b>seigneuries</b>,
        a series of narrow strips running from the riverbanks
        deep into the interior. The St. Lawrence
        valley is cut up uniformly, along a
        northwest–southeast orientation. <span style="mso-bidi-font-size: 12.0pt"><o:p>
        </o:p>
        </span>But we should not imagine that these grants, shaped like
        long rectangles, were all the same size. They varied
        according to the merits or the influence of the<span style="mso-spacerun: yes">&nbsp;
        </span>seigneur with the authorities.
@@ 8
A seigneury is in turn subdivided in the same way</b>.
        So it is made up of a<img border="0" src="images/manoirgiffard.jpg" align="right" hspace="20" vspace="20" width="250" height="146"> series of narrow strips
        perpendicular to the river. The most important part is of course
        the seigneur's personal domain. Here is the <b>manor house</b>
        of Robert Giffard in his seigneury of Beauport.
@@ 9
Not far from it is
        usually the land of the <b>Fabrique</b> (parish council), where a church
        and a presbytery are built.</font>&nbsp; <font face="Times New Roman" size="3">Usually
        the seigneur sets aside a piece of land where the habitants can
        graze their animals, called the <b>Commune</b> (common). Then
        come the lands granted to the habitants, called rotures. We must
        not forget that these lands are grants, which means
        the seigneur keeps his right of ownership over them. <span style="mso-bidi-font-size: 12.0pt"><o:p>
        But <b>the settler may sell his land</b>, with his
        seigneur's permission, for the value of the work he has done on it and the buildings
        he has put up.
@@ 10
A
        seigneury generally had no village, since the aim was for
        most of the land to be farmed. The lack of craftsmen
        was soon felt, and five or six <b> villages</b> came into
        being, one of the largest being Fargy, whose 1663 census
        counted about 63 people. A village was usually surrounded
        by a palisade and had a church, a public market, a cemetery
        and a flour mill.
@@ 11
In theory, the seigneur
        had to grant land to every habitant who asked for it and
        build a <b>grist mill</b> on his fief. In practice, the mill
        was built only when there were enough people to make it
        pay. The censitaire would then grind his grain there and pay a milling
        fee. A seigneur who granted land expected to receive
        burdensome dues such as an annual <b>rent</b> (the cens et rentes) and
        the &quot;<b>tax</b>&quot; (the lods et ventes) if the habitant sold his
        land. He also had a right of <b>corvée</b> that let him demand
        days of labour from his censitaire.
@@ 12
It is estimated that the censitaire
        paid his seigneur about 65 livres a year, which was
        a large sum when you consider that a day labourer could earn between
        120 and 200 livres a year and a skilled worker around 300 to
        400 livres. The seigneur also enjoyed a number of <b>honorary
        rights</b>, such as having a well-placed pew in church
        and being first to receive communion and the blessed bread.

@@ 13
Though
        in the early days the seigneurial regime provided <b> conditions
        of mutual help</b> that favoured the colony's development, it <b> gradually
        became an obstacle to the progress</b> of the country as a whole. The habitant was

        regime was not abolished until 1854, which for a time held back
        the colony's economic development, since it <b> hindered the
        mobility of land</b> and the development of capitalism.

AT WORK
@@ 21
The first job in settling in is <b>
        clearing the land</b>, which is especially hard work:felling the standing trees,
        pulling up the small and medium stumps, burning the brush, clearing the
        ground of stones.&nbsp; Slow work: it has been calculated that a settler working alone
        needs a year to clear and make ready for farming
        an area of an arpent and a half (half a hectare), and even then
        for some time the crops have to be grown among the big stumps he
        could not remove and must leave to rot.

The
        first houses of New France
@@ 27
A
      large number of the immigrants who landed in New France came from the
      Perche, a small region south of Lower Normandy. They arrived
      with their know-how and built houses like those of
      their home region: a rectangular plan, chimneys set in the gables,
      roof slopes pitched at 45° to 55°, asymmetrical
      openings and so on. In this way they strongly influenced
      the colony's architecture.
@@ 28
The first
      dwellings were mostly built of wood</b>. Wood stands up
      well to the cold but is a constant fire hazard. <b>Mathieu,
      in 1651</b>, surely rebuilt his house in wood, but the <b>following
      generations</b> hoped to guard against fire by building <b>stone
      houses</b> like those found in France. This type of dwelling
      did not suit the local climate, however: stone conducts the
      cold, and with the first frosts the mortar holding it crumbles. What is
      more, the house rested directly on the ground and shifted during the spring
      thaw.
@@ 29
To solve
      this problem, a new mortar was developed that could withstand cold
      and heat alike. A stone base, wider than the footprint of the building,
      was also built, called the <b>solage</b> (foundation).
      Two further steps were then needed to protect against the harsh
      winters: <b>renchaussage</b>,
      which meant banking straw and earth around the foundation, and <b>gasparde,
      </b>a clay-based plaster
      applied to the inside walls.
@@ 30
These <b>first
      dwellings</b>  <b> were small</b> and therefore easier
      to heat. They usually measured six metres by eight.&nbsp;
      The depth was usually set by the span of the beams.
      When the family grew, an extension was added at one end.
@@ 31
The houses
      all had an <b>attic</b> reached by a ladder or
      a small staircase<span style="mso-fareast-font-family: Times New Roman; mso-bidi-font-family: Times New Roman; mso-ansi-language: FR-CA; mso-fareast-language: FR; mso-bidi-language: AR-SA"><img border="0" src="images/maison1.jpg" align="right" hspace="20" vspace="20" width="194" height="131"></span> and at least two chimneys, since one was not enough to
      keep warm during the winter. The bread oven was set next to the hearth.&nbsp;
      Care was also taken to have no windows on the northeast side;
      those on the west and south sides were covered
      with oiled parchment.
@@ 32
T<b>he inside
      of the houses</b> often came down to <b>a single room</b> that served at
      once as kitchen, living room and bedroom. There was
      always a large <b> fireplace</b> with a hearth of flat stones, below
      which stood the pots and pans. The <b>furniture</b>, for its
      part, was very simple: a table, five or six chairs,
      a few chests, a bread bin and a &quot;cabane&quot; bed closed in with
      doors or curtains.&nbsp; So the whole family lived in this one room,
      where they slept, ate, worked and played.

FARM EQUIPMENT
@@ 34
At first there were not
        <b> barns</b>
        everywhere, and they were very small, about 44 feet by 22.&nbsp; What
        is surprising about the farms is that, in a country with harsh winters,
        the <b>dispersed layout</b> was adopted from the 17th century on, rather than the
        enclosed farmstead used in France. An enclosed farmstead, with its inner courtyard
        better sheltered from the wind, makes it easier to get from one
        building to another.&nbsp; Was the aim to reduce the risk of fire
        spreading to every part of the farm?&nbsp; Be that as it may,
        despite the settlers' varied origins,&nbsp; from the very
        start the first generation adopted the dispersed layout, and,
        despite the problems it poses, it is the layout the following
        generations kept.
@@ 35
The settler owned the <b> traditional
        tools</b>: the two-wheeled cart and the wheeled plough.&nbsp;
        The <b> livestock</b> was minimal: an ox used as a draught animal, a milk
        cow, pigs and poultry.&nbsp; Sheep were rare, since
        fur, easily found locally, replaced wool.&nbsp; The
        horse did not arrive until 1665 and was not at first within reach of
        &quot;ordinary folk&quot;.
@@ 36
The settler <b> grew</b> mainly bread
        wheat, but also barley, oats, rye, peas and even the
        Indigenous blé d'Inde (corn).&nbsp; He sowed kitchen-garden plants: lettuces,
        radishes, carrots, beets, turnips, cabbages and pumpkin, another
        Indigenous plant.
@@ 37
Mathieu and Sébastienne practise <b>
        subsistence farming</b>: for the family, with a small surplus to sell
        to buy the goods they cannot produce.&nbsp; The couple is
        the very foundation of life on a farm.&nbsp; They work side by side,
        the woman as hard as the man, even when pregnant.&nbsp;
        For three quarters of the year they toil non-stop from sunrise to
        sunset.

THE SETTLER'S CLOTHING
@@ 39
The immigrant of the colony's early
        years dressed as in France, while taking care to adapt
        more and more to the new conditions of the country.&nbsp; On his feet he might
        wear &quot;French&quot; shoes, with heels, made
        in France and protected by mules (like our
        overshoes), or &quot;sauvages&quot; (Indigenous-style) shoes, without heels,
        adapted from Indigenous footwear.
@@ 40
For <b>work</b>, he wore wooden
        clogs.&nbsp; Clothing was basic, including shirts of
        coarse linen down to mid-thigh so as not to hamper
        movement at work, for instance during mowing.&nbsp; If we
        wore such a rough shirt for half an hour today,
        our skin would be irritated.
@@ 41
Women always wore a coif.&nbsp; The first generation of settlers dressed
        in imported cloth.&nbsp; But we notice among them
        additions due to <b>Indigenous influence</b>: beaver mittens and
        muffs, fur caps of bearskin, breeches of moose
        hide, &quot;sauvages&quot; shoes.
