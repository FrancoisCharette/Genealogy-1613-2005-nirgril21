Champlain et les débuts de Québec
@@ title
Champlain and the beginnings of Quebec
@@ 1
Fur trading post
@@ 2
On his voyages Cartier sees clearly
        that there are furs in Canada, nothing more.&nbsp; But by the time of <b><font size="3" color="#464328">
        Champlain</font></b> the fur trade has become very
        lucrative.&nbsp;&nbsp; The King of France allows businessmen,
        grouped in a private company, to hold a monopoly on&nbsp; this trade
        in return for a share of the profits.&nbsp; The company must
        also see to bringing in settlers and missionaries to
        evangelize the Indigenous peoples.&nbsp; Champlain works for <b>
        <font size="3" color="#464328">
        De Monts</font></b>, a Huguenot merchant.&nbsp;&nbsp; After the failure
        of a colony in Acadia, De Monts asks Champlain
        to go into the interior of the continent and set up a trading
        post there.&nbsp; This takes a great deal of capital, steadier
        exploitation of natural resources, a permanent settlement and
        year-round staff.
@@ 3
Champlain arrives in 1608
@@ 4
In 1608 De Monts has <b>
        <font size="3" color="#464328">
        the Abitation</font></b> built at his own expense, and he remains the owner of Quebec until
        1613.&nbsp; He is the true founder of Quebec.&nbsp; Champlain still hopes
        to find the route to the Indies and thinks that from the top of
        Cap-aux-Diamants he can control trade with Asia.
@@ 5
So Champlain settles in
      Quebec with twenty-seven men, masons, carpenters and tradesmen
      employed by the company.&nbsp; They are called the <b><font color="#464328">hivernants (winterers)
@@ 6
Champlain takes care
      to stockpile food as well as building <i>the abitation</i>: a small wooden
      fort with an enclosure protected by cannons.&nbsp; An
      imposing structure for the time and place, it has three main buildings: one
      for the craftsmen, one for Champlain, and storehouses for supplies
      and trade goods.&nbsp; The French successfully sow
      wheat the very first summer.&nbsp; The fishing is miraculous, and
      waterfowl and caribou abound.
@@ 7
Surviving the winter
@@ 8
Geological research
        in the 20th century has shown that northern America went through a little ice age
        lasting 300 years, from 1500 to 1820.&nbsp; This
        is thought to have meant harsh winters and more rain in summer.
@@ 9
The climate is harsh in
        New France;&nbsp; Champlain's first winter in Quebec is
        merciless: twenty people out of&nbsp; twenty-eight&nbsp; die, thirteen of
        scurvy and seven of dysentery.&nbsp; Champlain is helpless; he does not know
        the remedy for scurvy that Cartier had received from the
        Indigenous people.&nbsp; Is this settlement doomed to fail like
        all the others in New France so far (Tadoussac,
        Acadia...)?
@@ 10
Surviving the winter becomes the only
        priority</font></b>.&nbsp; Without that, no permanent settlement is
        possible.&nbsp; The French realize they must watch and
        imitate the Indigenous people.&nbsp; So they dig pits to
        keep corn, fruit and vegetables.&nbsp; They wrap themselves
        in fur coats and fur caps, walk on
        snowshoes, and go hunting and fishing.&nbsp; This new way of
        life helps them a great deal to adapt and to beat the winter.&nbsp; The
        winters of 1610 and 1611 claim no victims, and Champlain
        understands that it is a poor diet that kills, not the
        cold.
@@ 11
Quebec nonetheless remains nothing more than a
        trading post where the Indigenous people come to exchange
        furs for goods.&nbsp; No one really wants to
        settle there.
@@ 12
The first settler
@@ 13
It is only with the arrival of <b><font size="3" color="#464328"> Louis
        Hébert</font></b> and his family that Quebec becomes a true
        colony.&nbsp; Champlain had known Louis Hébert since they were
        in Acadia together.&nbsp; He persuades him to come and settle in Quebec
        in 1617.&nbsp; Louis Hébert is hired by the Company, to which he must
        sell his surplus crops.&nbsp; Being an apothecary, he must
        also treat the other engagés free of charge.&nbsp; He may not
        trade in furs, since the company wants no competition.&nbsp;
        Champlain counts on his example to encourage other settlers
        to come.
@@ 14
Champlain
        travels the country, draws maps, goes farther up the St. Lawrence
        than Cartier and reaches the Great Lakes.&nbsp; He does
        everything he can to convince the Company of the country's riches and
        the need to invest in settlers.
@@ 15
In 1618 Champlain presents &quot;<b>  <font color="#464328">the inventory of Canada's natural resources</font></b>
        <font color="#464328"> </font>
        &quot; to the French chamber
        of commerce.&nbsp; <b><font size="3" color="#464328">Richelieu</font></b>, minister to King Louis XIII, wants
        a Catholic company.&nbsp; He pushes for the founding of the <font size="3"><b><font color="#464328" face="Times New Roman"> Compagnie
        des Cent Associés</font></b> </font> (Company of One Hundred Associates) in 1627.&nbsp; This powerful company takes
        charge of Canada.&nbsp; Yet that same year no ship reaches
        the colony, which depends entirely on France for its
        survival, since there are not enough settlers to grow
        food.
@@ 16
Why so few settlers?
@@ 17
The company sees no advantage in it:
        the Indigenous people bring enough furs to the trading
        post.&nbsp; As for the missionaries, they prefer a few good
        Catholic settlers who set an example for the people they want
        to convert, rather than many settlers, some of whom might be
        bad characters.&nbsp; <font color="#464328"><b>The population of the Habitation will never
        exceed 100 people.
@@ 18
17th-century France is the
        most populous country in Europe, and people there believe that the more
        populous a country is, the more powerful it is, since it is easier to raise an
        army.&nbsp; France does not want to lose population; that would
        remain its great fear until the 18th century.
@@ 19
While in the <b> <font color="#464328"> English colonies</font></b> the
        population is already 5,000, then 10,000 (the town of
        Boston is founded in one go by the arrival of 2,000 people),
        Quebec's population grows drop by drop.
@@ 20
In the race
        for colonies France cannot win, because it excludes the
        Protestants, against whom it is waging an internal war, and because for France
        Canada is merely a fur-trading venture that
        needs few people.&nbsp; It does not aim to build a strong
        colonial core, whereas the English in New England and the
        Dutch in <b><font color="#464328"> New York</font></b> succeed in doing so.
@@ 21
The English attack
@@ 22
War breaks out between France and
        England in 1627, and the King of England, Charles I, takes the opportunity
        to enrich himself by seizing the fur trade.&nbsp;
        He commissions the <b> <font color="#464328" size="3"> Kirke brothers</font></b> to take the trading posts of
        New France.&nbsp; Their first attempt, in 1628,&nbsp;
        fails,&nbsp; but they come back in 1629.&nbsp; They take
        Tadoussac, an outport of Quebec (finding the navigation
        up to Quebec too risky, ships stopped there and people
        continued their journey by small boat).&nbsp; The English burn the
        crops,&nbsp; capture Jesuits and massacre people of the
        Gaspé.&nbsp; Then, better navigators than the French, they
        appear before Quebec.&nbsp; Champlain cannot believe his
        eyes:&nbsp; he himself had never dared sail up the St. Lawrence beyond
        Tadoussac.&nbsp; On July 19, 1629, he has to resign himself to surrendering
        to the English.
@@ 23
The Kirke brothers' task is not
        hard, since the winters of 1628 and 1629 had greatly weakened the
        population.&nbsp; The ships meant to supply the people
        had been intercepted by the English, and famine
        was rife.&nbsp; Some had taken refuge with the Algonquins
        so as not to starve; others were reduced to eating
        roots.&nbsp; So they did not have the strength to resist the Kirke
        brothers.
@@ 24
Louis Hébert's family and a few
        interpreters decide to stay in the country.&nbsp; On July 24, 1629,
        watching their compatriots leave Quebec, they think it is the end of Champlain's great dream.&nbsp; During the
        crossing, on meeting another ship, Champlain learns that France is
        victorious and that the war was already over when England
        seized Quebec.&nbsp; But it is too late... almost the entire
        population is on board with him.
@@ 25
Three years later the North American
        colony is returned to France by <b> <font color="#464328"> the Treaty of
        Saint-Germain-en-Laye.
@@ 26
The real beginnings of Quebec
@@ 27
Champlain takes
        Quebec back
@@ 28
The Compagnie des Cent Associés then begins
        its work of colonization.&nbsp; Entrepreneurs such as Robert
        Giffard and the Juchereau brothers gradually recruit settlers.&nbsp;&nbsp;By 1635
        New France has four hundred inhabitants.&nbsp; A little
        infrastructure appears: missionaries found the Jesuit
        College, and various religious communities settle, including the
        Ursulines and the Hospitallers, who devote themselves to education and
        care of the sick.
@@ 29
Throughout these first faltering steps
        of Quebec, <b> <font color="#464328" size="4" face="Times New Roman"> Mathieu
        Choret</font></b>, born around 1620 in France, is a young child.&nbsp; He
        comes on the scene in the mid-1640s.&nbsp; Quebec is then only just
        beginning to develop, but it will never stop again.
