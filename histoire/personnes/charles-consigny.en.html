Charles Consigny

Son of Arsène Consigny and Amanda Thibaudeau, of Quebec City. A gunner throughout the First World War, then a soldier again in 1940.

In Nicole's words

Charles thus lost his father at 3 and his mother at 14. The family legend said that he and his brother Antoine were placed in an orphanage and that an uncle, acting as guardian, squandered the young Consigny orphans' property. At the Consignys'
When he enlisted for the First World War in 1914, Charles reported eight years of schooling and, as his trade, "steam fitter". He gave as a reference his sister Crescence, married since 1910 to Rolland Pleau and living in Saint-Sauveur, in Quebec City. At the Consignys'
In August 1914, England went to war, and Canada along with it. Charles was one of the thousands of young men who flocked to the recruiting centres. They enlisted out of a taste for adventure. The lure of Europe was very strong and everyone was saying the war would be over by Christmas, so why not? After a few weeks of training at Camp Valcartier, Charles enlisted on September 26, 1914, and sailed for Europe in October. We have a copy of his attestation paper. For 4 years and 8 months Charles Consigny took part in the Great War in Europe. He belonged to the Canadian Artillery, 1st Canadian Heavy Battery; his service number was 43804 and he was a gunner. Here is his "discharge" certificate. According to the memories of his daughter Jacqueline, he had been "gassed". No doubt the severe migraines he suffered from for the rest of his life were the after-effects. It is also more than likely that Charles suffered from some degree of deafness, since all gunners were more or less affected. At the Consignys' — VIE
In August, as soon as the war began, Charles Consigny volunteered. He did basic military training at Camp Valcartier, then was transferred to England in October. The first Canadian contingent was sent to Bustard Camp on Salisbury Plain. More intensive training, the gradual arrival of equipment, and rain three days out of four: that was the soldiers' daily life. Charles was attached to the D.A.C., a supply unit. La der des ders (the war to end all wars) — Charles Consigny, 1914–1919 — resume
In February, Charles crossed to France. He was landed at St-Nazaire and made a two-day train journey to Hazebrouck, behind the front. At the end of the month Charles was put under arrest for fifteen days after being absent without leave for three days. In March Charles was transferred to the 1st Canadian Heavy Battery and became a gunner. In May he took part in the Battle of Aubers Ridge and the Battle of Festubert; in June, the Battle of Givenchy. There he was found guilty of a second offence, neglect of duty, and sentenced to five days' arrest. In September he was at the Battle of Loos. The winter was long. In April, Charles was hospitalized for two weeks with "Cervical Adenitis". At the end of June, Charles's unit was taken by train toward Amiens to take part in the Battle of the Somme. It began on July 1 and lasted more than five months, without any respite. More than a thousand salvoes were fired every day, day and night. La der des ders (the war to end all wars) — Charles Consigny, 1914–1919 — resume
From January 12 to 23, Charles had his first leave. A few weeks later he was hospitalized for a venereal disease. In early March he was transferred to a hospital in Le Havre, Normandy. He returned to his unit in May, in the Vimy area, where the fighting was fierce. In the fall the battle shifted to the Ypres salient. At the end of October, it was the Battle of Passchendaele for Charles. On December 23, Charles had a second leave. He visited Bordeaux. La der des ders (the war to end all wars) — Charles Consigny, 1914–1919 — resume
Charles returned to his unit on January 6. At the end of the month he was hospitalized for the third time. He was suffering from "lymphangilis" and was treated at Camiers, in the Pas-de-Calais, until February 13. Then he went back to the Vimy area, where the fighting was raging. On June 18 he was hospitalized again, along with several of his comrades, after an epidemic of "three days fever". He returned to his unit on June 24. In August he took part in the Battle of Arras. In September it was the Battle of the Canal du Nord. The fighting went on fiercely in October. November 11, the Armistice. At the end of the month, Charles and his unit marched all the way to Namur, in Belgium. Then they marched on, in short stages, toward Bonn in Germany. They arrived on December 16 and served there as an army of occupation. La der des ders (the war to end all wars) — Charles Consigny, 1914–1919 — resume
In January Charles had a leave. In February Charles's unit was back in Namur. On February 25 Charles was hospitalized with Spanish flu. He was transferred to a military hospital in England. On April 14 Charles was sent to a transit camp to wait to be shipped back to Canada. On May 14 he sailed from Liverpool, and he was in Quebec City on May 26. Charles was reunited with his family and his fiancée, Ézilda Côté, at the end of May 1919. He drew the balance of his army account, about $755, and the wedding preparations began. Ézilda lived in the Maisonneuve district, at 580 Bourbonnière. She was in half-mourning for her father, which is why she is dressed in black in the photos from this period. The engaged couple went up to Quebec City to be godparents, on June 10, to the first son of Crescence, Charles's sister. At the Consignys' — VIE
Charles and Ézilda were married in Très-St-Nom-de-Jésus parish in Montreal on July 28, 1919. On the marriage record Charles gave his trade as plumber. Their witnesses were Antoine, Charles's brother, and Omer Béchard, Ézilda's brother-in-law. That day she wore Charles's engagement present, a pair of teardrop pearl earrings. We still have these earrings. At the Consignys' — VIE
I suppose that after their wedding the couple lived for a few years with Ézilda's family. This is because there is no trace of the couple in the Lovell directories before 1925. Such living arrangements had long been common, and even after the Second World War, since young couples lacked the means. So they lived first at 580 Bourbonnière, then at 2055 Ste-Catherine, at 2709 Ontario, and at 2531 Ste-Catherine est. (N.B. these addresses date from before the city's renumbering and no longer match today's). We find a flat in the name of Consini Eug only from 1925 on, and it is at 2179 Létourneux. At the Consignys' — VIE
On his children's birth records, Charles gave his trade as plumber or day labourer. We know nothing more, except that finding work was not easy for men who had spent so long in Europe, and that the couple also had to get through the "Great Depression". We have a series of four postcards, sent probably in the 1920s, that reveal Charles's boredom and a certain distress. In them he mentions steps he was taking to go to Boston for some new training that would let him work like Rolland, his brother-in-law. Part of the text is illegible, so we do not really know what he had in mind... At the Consignys' — VIE
When the Second World War broke out, Charles returned to active service. He signed his enlistment form on June 3, 1940, in Montreal. He was attached to unit no. 4 A, Veterans Guard of Canada, with the service number D-107010. The couple kept in touch by correspondence, by letters or post cards. Being illiterate, Ézilda had to have Charles's letters read to her by someone else, and dictate her reply. The children took on that role as soon as they could read and write. In July 1940 Charles was in Quebec City, in September at Île-aux-Noix. In 1941 he was posted to Kingston At the Consignys' — VIE
Charles died there on October 19, 1941. His death certificate gives as the official cause 'thombois', that is, in all likelihood, a thrombosis (formation of clots in a blood vessel). But doubt would always remain in the family. In fact, Ézilda asked to see her husband one last time before the funeral. The military authorities refused to open the coffin. This refusal offended the family and led them to suppose that Charles's body might bear marks proving a death that was not natural! Since Charles's last posting was supposedly guarding German prisoners, could he have been killed during an escape or an escape attempt? At the Consignys' — VIE
