1891–1964

Ézilda Côté

Born in Quebec City, daughter of Gaudias Côté, a shoemaker, and Georgiana Pageau. Wife of Charles Consigny; she spends her life in Maisonneuve.

In Nicole's words

Ézilda Côté is born in Québec City, in the Saint-Sauveur district, on December 27, 1891. Her father, Gaudias, is a shoemaker. Ézilda has no schooling, but she can sign her name. The whole family moves to Montréal in 1903, to the new industrial town of Maisonneuve. They find work there and will spend the rest of their lives there. Ézilda works in a shoe factory until her marriage. Two parallel lives (Deux vies en parallèle)
Ézilda marries Charles Consigny on July 28, 1919, in Très-St-Nom-de-Jésus parish in Montréal. It is a simple wedding, as Ézilda is in mourning for her father. Charles has only been back home since the end of May; he had served in the war in Europe for nearly five years. The young couple lives in Maisonneuve, probably with Ézilda's mother, Georgianna, during the first years. Two parallel lives (Deux vies en parallèle)
Ézilda has five children, two of whom die in infancy. In 1925 the couple settles at 2179 Létourneux, Maisonneuve. It is a modest flat, where she raises her family with her husband, a day labourer. In 1940 Charles re-enlists in the army. As soon as they finish elementary school, the children go out to work. On fine summer Sundays, the family joins relatives and friends for picnics nearby, for example at the Botanical Garden, just around the corner. Two parallel lives (Deux vies en parallèle)
Ézilda loses her husband on October 19, 1941. He dies suddenly in Kingston, where he is working for the Canadian army. For the rest of her life she receives a widow's pension, which gives her a minimum of financial security. She stays in the same flat; her children help her by paying "room and board" and with the hardest daily chores (for example, carrying the firewood up to the second floor, since she had a wood stove for cooking and heating). Two parallel lives (Deux vies en parallèle)
From 1947 to 1964 Ézilda has sixteen grandchildren. She still lives on rue Létourneux. She devotedly helps her two daughters through their "relevailles" (the weeks of rest after childbirth) after each birth. Ézilda loves listening to the radio and often goes to the movies in the neighbourhood. She only buys herself a refrigerator or a TV at the very end of her life. Modern gifts don't interest her; she puts them away, still in their wrapping, at the back of the closet. For example, rather than an electric iron, she prefers her old irons, which she heats on her wood stove. Two parallel lives (Deux vies en parallèle)
Ézilda dies on October 17, 1964, in her flat in Montréal, where she lived alone. She is said to have died of breathing problems after some painting work she had undertaken herself. Two parallel lives (Deux vies en parallèle)
Ézilda told me herself that she wanted to become a nun. So she entered the convent, probably in Montreal, since that is where she lived from 1903 on. Some time later, she fell ill and the religious community's superiors sent her back to her family because, judged to be in fragile health and illiterate, she was of no use to the community. Ézilda took a job in a shoe factory in the neighbourhood and worked there until her marriage. We do not know how Charles and Ézilda met, or when they got engaged. The only clue we have is that the husband of Crescence Consigny, Charles's sister, was Rolland Pleau, a cousin of Ézilda's on her mother's side. We can suppose that this family tie led to Charles and Ézilda meeting. In all likelihood, Charles already knew Ézilda before leaving for Europe in 1914, since he wrote to her during the war and married her as soon as he got back in 1919. In those days, soldiers had no leave during their service, so Charles must have known Ézilda before going to war. At the Consignys' — FAM_COTE
Ézilda lived on a "veteran's widow's" pension paid monthly by the federal government. This pension saved her from destitution, because at that time the income security system did not yet exist. Only the federal old-age pension had been introduced, but it was paid at 70, while Ézilda was only 50 when she was widowed. Her son Arsène, who now had the status of "only son of a widow", was exempted from going to war in Europe even though, like most young men of the time, he had received military training (at Grosse Île, opposite Montmagny, among other places). At the Consignys' — VIE
Ézilda was not left alone, however, because of a housing shortage (no new housing was built during the war, and many weddings took place at its end): newlyweds at that time lived with their parents. So Fabiola and Elphège spent five years with Elphège's mother, and Jacqueline, after a short stay with her mother-in-law, went back to her mother's. She and her husband Gérard spent more than a year there and had their first child, Nicole, there. And so Ézilda became a grandmother. She would have 16 grandchildren. She took charge of the "relevailles" (lying-in) of her two daughters after every birth. That meant staying at their homes, taking charge of the household and the children, and caring for the newborn and its mother. It was a custom of mother-daughter help in which she never once failed, right up to the birth of her last grandson, François, a few months before she died in 1964. At the Consignys' — VIE
