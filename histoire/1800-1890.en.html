May 28, 1812War

War of 1812: an elite militia is created

Jacques was very probably a militiaman.

Nicole's text
So Jacques was, very probably, a militiaman. What were his political views? As today, most people probably wanted nothing more than a quiet life for their family. Putting bread on the table was not easy in those days, because harvests were often poor. Not to mention the epidemics of picote (smallpox) and scarlet fever (1783), smallpox (1784), cholera (1832-1854) and typhus (1847), which claimed thousands of victims. original page
During the War of 1812, an elite militia was created on May 28, 1812, when Governor George Prevost imposed conscription on 2,000 militiamen chosen by lot. Four battalions were to lend a hand to the regular army. Other major call-ups followed, until the end of the war. Jacques was then 35 years old, newly married and father of a first son born in 1811. original page

Cholera epidemic

Nicole's text
In 1832 a cholera epidemic from Europe, the same one Jean Giono describes in his novel Le Hussard sur le toit (The Horseman on the Roof), claimed thousands of victims in Canada. That summer, on Place d'Armes, a shooting in which three Canadiens were killed by the British army clearly showed the climate of political tension in the country. original page

1837War

The rebellions of 1837-1838

Their first child, Charles, is born in Saint-Jérôme in April 1837; that autumn the Patriotes' Rebellion breaks out.

Nicole's text
The first child of Jacques and Théotiste was born in April 1837. The following autumn came the Patriotes' Rebellion. Several more children followed, including two sets of twins. The family moved to Saint-Sauveur-des- Monts in 1853, where the couple's last two children would be born. Jacques was a farmer and day labourer. These occupations appear in the baptism records of the couple's many children. Notably, in most of these records the priest added the words "father absent". So it seems Jacques often worked far from home, whatever the season. Let's not forget that at the time a child was baptized very soon after birth, the same day if possible. original page
During the rebellions of 1837-1838, Jacques and Théotiste were a young couple in Saint-Jérôme with a small child. We don't know their thoughts or actions, but there can be no doubt that these grave events had a great impact on their lives. Did they side with the rebels or with the loyal militiamen? A significant part of a people reputed to be isolated, backward and illiterate was then swept, more or less consciously, by a current of thought that stood at the forefront of the Western world. original page

Nicole's text
Jacques died in Montréal on August 5, 1869. His burial record says he was a day labourer, widower of Marie Varin, living in Notre-Dame parish and aged 98 1/2 (he was really closer to 88 1/2. The priest got his arithmetic wrong, or was misinformed. It is common, in official records, to find approximate or incorrect ages). 1870

1871Home

Jacques and his family settle in Saint-Henri

First on rue Renaud, then rue Saint-Antoine from 1872 (no. 1100 when house numbering begins in 1876). Five generations of Charettes will live in this district for seventy years.

Nicole's text
Starting with the 1871-1872 Lovell* directory, we find Jacques and his family in Saint-Henri. His occupation is laborer. In 1871 he was on rue Renaud. From 1872 he lived on rue Saint-Antoine. Note that in those years no house numbers were listed in the directories, only the street name. original page
In 1876 house numbering began, and Jacques was at 1100 Saint-Antoine. His son Alphonse, married since February 1873, and his young family lived with them. That makes all the more sense since 1873 saw the start of a worldwide economic crisis that would bring bankruptcies, factory closures and unemployment for several years. In 1878 we find Jacques, carpenter, at 1180 Saint-Antoine. The following year he was back at 1100 Saint-Antoine, where Alphonse was still living. In 1880 Alphonse moved close by, to 1098 Saint-Antoine. His parents went on living at 1100 Saint-Antoine. original page

February 25, 1873Marriage

Alphonse Charette marries Rosianne Miron

At Saint-Joseph church, rue Richmond. The couple moves in with Alphonse's parents at 1100 rue Saint-Antoine.

Nicole's text
On Tuesday, February 25, 1873, in the church of Saint-Joseph parish, rue Richmond in Montréal, Alphonse Charette marries Rosianne Miron. The groom's witness is his father Jacques, and the bride's is her stepfather Louis Casavant. Alphonse is twenty-two and Rosianne sixteen. Neither of them can sign the marriage record. original page
The young couple moves in with Alphonse's parents, in Saint-Henri-des-Tanneries, at 1100 rue Saint-Antoine. Also living in this flat are Alphonse's youngest brothers and sisters and, now and then, an uncle or another relative "boards" there for a while. So one can speak of a family clan. People are used to "squeezing in"; privacy is out of the question. It is only in 1880 that Alphonse and Rosianne and their children move into the flat next door, at 1098 Saint-Antoine. original page

The conclusion names the youngest daughter "Alice, born 1865"; the census line and the list of children call her Eliza/Élisa (born February 3, 1865).

Nicole's text
Conclusion: combining other sources, we can say that in 1881 Jacques was living in Saint-Henri with his wife and his three youngest children, Hélène, born in 1861, Alice, born in 1865, and Joseph, born in 1863. The two young women worked in a factory, one making sheets, the other as an apprentice dressmaker. There was no shortage of factories in Saint-Henri in the second half of the 19th century. original page
In 1882 Jacques moved to 1102 Saint-Antoine, then in 1887 he went back to 1100 Saint-Antoine. His name appears in the Lovell* directories until 1892. Note that an occupation is always listed next to his name (laborer or carpenter). He never retired. Remember that there was not yet any social security or old-age pension. original page
