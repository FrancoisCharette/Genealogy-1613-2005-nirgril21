Robert Caron is probably born in Rouen

The earliest known ancestor of the family to come to New France.

Nicole's text
Robert Caron, probably born on July 22, 1613, in Rouen, France, sails for New France on one of the four ships of the Compagnie Cheffault-Rozee (a company founded for 5 years, based in Rouen, and charged with carrying immigrants to the St. Lawrence valley). Robert Caron thus arrives with Robert Giffard, seigneur of Beauport, and all the first settlers who came with him: Jean Côté, Jean Bourbon, Noël Langlois, Zacharie Cloutier. original page

June 4, 1634Home

Robert Caron arrives in New France with Robert Giffard

He is in the first group brought over by Giffard, hired for three years at 75 livres a year.

Nicole's text
Of all our ancestral lines known to date, Robert Caron is the first to arrive in New France, on June 4, 1634 (he was in the first group brought over by Robert Giffard). original page
Each immigrant is hired for 3 years at 75 livres a year (with room and board). After that he is free to return to France or to settle in the country. In 1636, Pierre Le Gardeur de Repentigny grants Robert Caron a piece of land at Longue Pointe, on the côte de Beaupré, near the Sault Montmorency (Montmorency Falls). Robert is an entrepreneur: in the registers of the notaires (notaries) of the time, we find deeds in his name for sales of timber for large sums and the sale of a lot in the town of Québec to the Ursulines (a lot "ten toises by five toises", a toise being about two metres). original page

October 1, 1634Marriage

Hélène Desportes marries Guillaume Hébert

Hélène Desportes, the first baby of European settlers born alive in New France, is a foremother of the Lemay line. Widowed in 1639, she marries Noël Morin on January 9, 1640; their eldest daughter Agnès is born in 1641.

Nicole's text
Hélène Desportes was the first baby of European settlers to be born alive in New France. Her parents, Pierre Desportes and Françoise Langlois, from Normandy, arrived in Québec in 1619. Hélène was born there on July 7, 1620. Her godfather was Samuel de Champlain, and her godmother Hélène Boullé, his wife. Hélène Desportes was the niece of Abraham Martin, who would give his first name to the great park on the heights of Québec and to the hill that links the Upper Town and the Lower Town. original page
On October 1, 1634, Hélène married Guillaume Hébert, son of Louis Hébert and Marie Rollet, the first settler and the first schoolteacher of New France, and also the first Frenchwoman to set foot on the soil of Québec, in 1617. original page
Hélène remarried on January 9, 1640, in Québec, to Noël Morin, 39, son of Claude Morin and Jeanne Moreau, of Saint-Étienne-de-Brie-Comte-Robert, Seine-et-Marne. original page
They had twelve children, including our foremother, their eldest daughter Agnès, born January 21, 1641, in Québec. Agnès married first, in 1653, Nicolas Gaudry dit Bourbonnière, 32. Then, as her second husband, she married Ignace Bonhomme dit Beaupré. original page

1642Home

Fearing the Iroquois, Robert Caron sells his land and settles in Québec

He settles on the côteau Sainte-Geneviève; in 1654 he sells all his properties in Québec.

Nicole's text
In 1642 Robert, fearing the Iroquois, not without reason, sells his land to his neighbour Guillaume Couillard for 150 livres. He settles in Québec on the côteau Sainte-Geneviève (Sainte-Geneviève hillside), later to be called « le Belvédère »; he clears land to make an opening and keep a better eye on the movements of the Iroquois. Timber is his main source of income. Several settlers of Norman origin had settled in this area, which is located where the present-day Collège des Jésuites stands, at the corner of René-Lévesque and Joffre. original page
For a reason known only to himself, in 1654 Robert Caron sells all his properties in Québec. His land on the côteau Sainte-Geneviève is sold to Charles D'Ailleboust, the governor's nephew, for the considerable sum of 1700 livres. A few months earlier, Robert had bought from Julien Fortin dit Bellefontaine*, for 500 livres, a farm five arpents wide by one league deep on the côte de Beaupré, at Sainte-Anne-du-Petit-Cap, about a mile east of the present-day basilica of Sainte-Anne. original page

1643Work

Mathieu Choret arrives in Canada as an engagé of the Jesuits

From the parish of Notre-Dame-de-Cougnes in La Rochelle. He is in Trois-Rivières by November 1645.

Nicole's text
Mathieu Choret arrived in New France in 1643 as an engagé of the Jesuits, with a three-year contract. original page
The first time the records mention him in the country, he is at Trois-Rivières, working as an engagé for the Jesuits. He earns a hundred livres a year. According to the Journal des Jésuites, he had been there since November 1645, replacing the settler Pierre Gonthier, who had gone back to Quebec, brought by Robert Hache, to do the cooking there " in place of our brothers, who found it too hard". Mathieu probably also spent time in the Hochelaga area (since in his 1647 marriage contract he says he lives in Montréal)? original page
According to several web sources, including the Fichier Origine, Mathieu comes from the parish of Notre-Dame-de-Cougnes in La Rochelle. This parish is the oldest in the town, dating from the 12th century. Since the town's churches, including Notre-Dame, had been destroyed in 1568 during the Wars of Religion, Mathieu was probably baptized in the Sainte-Marguerite chapel, where the people of La Rochelle worshipped for many years. original page

October 31, 1646Home

Mathieu Choret sails back to France

Nicole's text
At the end of his three-year contract, Mathieu takes ship for France on Wednesday, October 31, 1646 (Journal des Jésuites). An engagé's contract included his passage both ways. That day two ships leave Quebec; on the first is a Jesuit, Father Quentin, with Robert Hache. M. de Maisonneuve, M. Giffard and M. Tronquet, all important men in the colony, are also on board. On the second ship are the sons of M. de Repentigny, M. Couillard, M. March 6, 1649Home

Land-grant contract for Mathieu's land in the Seigneury of Notre-Dame-des-Anges

After a year as an engagé, Mathieu stays loyal to the Jesuits, who grant him land at the eastern end of their seigneury, near Beauport. It would lie today near the church of Giffard.

Nicole's text
Mathieu Choret, back from France in 1647 with his new wife, has a one-year contract as an engagé with the Jesuits. When the time comes for him to settle on land of his own, he stays loyal to the Jesuits and chooses their Seigneury of Notre-Dame-des-Anges. This seigneury lies north of the Saint-Charles River and stretches east to Robert Giffard's seigneury (Beauport). Since the land near Quebec was all taken, the Jesuits grant him land at the eastern end of their seigneury, near their farm. The contract is signed in March 1649, and it reads: "three arpents of land frontage on the Great River Saint Lawrence of New France and four leagues deep into the woods, ..." The Moulin brook crosses part of Mathieu's land. original page
When Mathieu Choret comes back to New France in 1647 with his wife Sébastienne Veillon, the lands near Québec are all taken, so the Jesuits grant him land at the eastern end of their seigneury, near their farm. The contract is signed in March 1649, and it reads: "three arpents of land fronting on the Great River Saint Lawrence of New France and four leagues deep into the woods, ..." The ruisseau du Moulin runs straight across part of Mathieu's land. This 8.8 km stream rises at the Montagne des Roches and flows into the bay of Beauport. original page
The documents say that Mathieu's land was roughly where the Giffard church stands today. That parish dates from 1914, when it was split off from Beauport parish. original page

1660

June 4, 1660Family

Marie Caron, Robert's eldest daughter, is abducted by Hurons allied with the Iroquois

Nicole's text
On June 4, 1660, Marie Caron (the eldest daughter), who had married Jean Picard on July 28, 1656, is abducted by Hurons allied with the Iroquois, together with her daughter Louise (born September 29, 1659) and three settlers' children she was looking after. Her husband was sowing the fields with neighbours (people banded together to defend themselves better in case of attack). Alerted, Governor d'Argenson sends a party of French and Algonquins to search for the young woman and the children. They flush out the kidnappers at Pointe de Lévy two days later. Marie, lying in the canoe, hears friendly voices, raises her head and is struck by a bullet that also kills the child she is holding in her arms. She is taken to the hospital, where she dies two days later. Her little daughter is raised by her Picard grandparents. original page

October 16, 1690War

Admiral Phips attacks Quebec; his militiamen land at Beauport

The Beauport militia, led by Nicolas Juchereau, is the first to face the enemy, on October 18.

Nicole's text
On October 16, 1690, William Phips, a 40-year-old native of Maine, took up a strong position before the town of Quebec aboard his flagship, the Six Friends. He commanded 34 ships, 5 of them armed with 106 cannons, and 2,300 sailors and militiamen. This army was made up of Americans, not Englishmen, because England, having suffered some naval defeats at the hands of the French in Europe, sent its orders to its colony but could not supply it with soldiers. Five months earlier, with only 7 ships and 450 men, Phips had demanded and obtained, without firing a shot, the surrender of Port-Royal in Acadia. From this event, and from the attack on Quebec, came the birth of a purely American patriotic feeling. New England settlers united for the first time and, without help from the mother country, took the defence of their lands into their own hands and went to attack the enemy on its own territory. original page
And it was precisely at Beauport, on the flats, that 1,400 of Phips's militiamen landed on October 18, 1690. So it fell to the Beauport militia to face the enemy first. Nicolas Juchereau, at the head of some sixty men, stopped them from going any farther and drove them back to La Canardière. This action took place very close to the home of Pierre Parent (Pierre Choret's godfather?) and the other settlers along the shore. The other militias came as reinforcements. The battles fought on October 18, 19 and 20 thus took place near baby Pierre Choret's home. original page

August 2, 1694Marriage

Jean Dionne marries Marie-Charlotte Mignot

Dionne ancestors of Alphonsine Gagnon (through her mother Ombéline Dionne).

Nicole's text
At 24, Jean Dionne marries at Château-Richer, on August 2, 1694, Marie-Charlotte Mignot, daughter of the late Jean Mignot-dit-Châtillon (1628-80) and of Louyse Cloutier (1631-99). Jean Dionne's wife is said to have been baptized at Beauport on February 2, 1671, but the 1681 census lists her at La Bouteillerie (Rivière-Ouelle) under the name Charlotte, aged 11. … First settled at Beauport, the Mignot-Châtillon family had left for Grande-Anse soon after 1674, then finally tried to settle at Rivière-Ouelle. original page
