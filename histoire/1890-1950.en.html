1895Home

Alphonse Charette Sr. moves into his big stone house at 1106 rue Saint-Antoine

A mason turned contractor (listed in the 1894 Lovell directory), he also owns several apartment blocks. The house hosts countless family gatherings.

Nicole's text
Alphonse works as a mason. At first he works for others; then, in his mid-forties, he becomes a masonry contractor. The first mention of this appears in the Lovell street directory of 1894. He builds many cut-stone houses, and even churches and other buildings. original page
He also builds a fine big stone house for his family at 1106 rue Saint-Antoine. They move in in 1895, and little by little it becomes the house of the Pater Familias, a role Alphonse fills happily, since he is clearly very proud of his family. This house is the setting for countless family gatherings to mark the happy and the sad moments. It is said that the basement is specially fitted out; Alphonse even puts in a complete kitchen there to make things easier for the women, who can cook in comfort while staying "with the company". The table is big enough to seat as many people as possible. There is a piano, others play the fiddle, and people have a good time at these parties. They sing, dance and play cards. Alphonse and Rosianne love to gather family and friends around them and to entertain them well. (Story passed on by Ghislaine, who heard it from Rita and Fleurette Lavoie, Rosario's cousins) original page
In addition to his trade, Alphonse gradually becomes an important property owner in Saint-Henri. He owns several apartment blocks; he looks after managing and maintaining them and the well-being of his tenants. In the marriage records of two of his sons, in 1907, Alphonse is listed as a "bourgeois" (a man of property). In the census of 1911 he declares himself a "rentier" (living on his income). In the same census several of his children, then newly married, are living in flats next to his. It is fair to say that Alphonse and Rosianne, despite their lack of schooling and the hardships they faced, built an enviable family and financial comfort. Alphonse dies on April 25, 1919. In 1928 the city changes the street numbering, and 1106 becomes 4230 rue Saint-Antoine. Rosianne lives there until her death on April 26, 1935. original page

February 26, 1896Death

Accidental death of Arsène Consigny, Charles's father

A mechanic, he is killed in Quebec City (Saint-Roch) while experimenting with acetylene, a new gas. Charles is three. Antoine, the last child, is born the following August 18.

Note

  • 31 — At the Consignys'

The page says "at the age of 31" (also in the quoted death notice); born October 14, 1865, he would have been 30.

Nicole's text
On the afternoon of February 26, 1896, Arsène died at the age of 31. While experimenting with acetylene gas, a brand-new product on the market, the two brothers caused a violent explosion that cost Arsène his life, seriously injured Nicolas and shook the neighbourhood. The two brothers were not new at this, for the month before, a minor explosion had already occurred. The newspaper L'Électeur of February 27, 1896, published a long article on "What acetylene is". Here are some extracts: " The accident that happened yesterday at the Consigny establishment makes acetylene more topical than ever in Quebec City, the new gas that may be destined to dethrone electricity for lighting, and even motive power, thanks to its low cost of manufacture. Mr. Consigny is the first in Quebec City to have tried to use this new gas. With the dynamo he uses to light his factory, Mr. N. Consigny succeeded in making his own carbide. original page

Marie-Anna and Rosario marry on January 16, 1917, in Saint-Zotique parish in Montréal. They are both under age; it is a simple wedding because Rosario is in mourning for his father. His guardian has to consent to the marriage. Marrying young is perfectly normal at the time, and young couples usually move in with one set of parents or the other. August 29, 1917Context

Conscription comes into force

The Military Service Act, passed by the House of Commons on July 24, 1917, comes into force once approved by the Senate. It sets off violent protests.

Nicole's text
August 29, 1917. Conscription. Under pressure to step up the war effort Canada had promised Great Britain, and no longer able to manage it with volunteers alone, Robert Borden's Conservative government has the Military Service Act passed by the House of Commons, 101 votes to 44, on July 24, 1917. It is mainly Liberals, including their leader Wilfrid Laurier, who oppose the measure. It is fairly well received across the country, except in Quebec, where it sparks several stormy demonstrations. With the Senate's approval, obtained on August 29, 1917, the new law comes into force. original page

October 1918Context

The Spanish flu

Nicole's text
October 1918. A terrible epidemic of (Spanish) flu sweeps across America from October 1918. Brought to Canada by soldiers returning from Europe, it spreads quickly and forces the authorities to take exceptional measures. The health department orders schools, cinemas, theatres and all public places closed. Panic grips the population; the death knell tolls day and night, there are so many dead to bury. Rosario surely fears for his young family, having lost his mother, brothers and sisters in epidemics early in the century. original page

November 11, 1918War

The Armistice; Charles's unit marches into Germany

After the battles of Arras and the Canal du Nord comes the Armistice. Charles and his unit march to Namur, then on to Bonn, arriving on December 16 as an army of occupation.

Nicole's text
Charles returned to his unit on January 6. At the end of the month he was hospitalized for the third time. He was suffering from "lymphangilis" and was treated at Camiers, in the Pas-de-Calais, until February 13. Then he went back to the Vimy area, where the fighting was raging. On June 18 he was hospitalized again, along with several of his comrades, after an epidemic of "three days fever". He returned to his unit on June 24. In August he took part in the Battle of Arras. In September it was the Battle of the Canal du Nord. The fighting went on fiercely in October. November 11, the Armistice. At the end of the month, Charles and his unit marched all the way to Namur, in Belgium. Then they marched on, in short stages, toward Bonn in Germany. They arrived on December 16 and served there as an army of occupation. original page

undatedFamily

Ézilda wanted to become a nun

She enters the convent, probably in Montreal, but falls ill and is sent back to her family.

Nicole's text
Ézilda told me herself that she wanted to become a nun. So she entered the convent, probably in Montreal, since that is where she lived from 1903 on. Some time later, she fell ill and the religious community's superiors sent her back to her family because, judged to be in fragile health and illiterate, she was of no use to the community. Ézilda took a job in a shoe factory in the neighbourhood and worked there until her marriage. We do not know how Charles and Ézilda met, or when they got engaged. The only clue we have is that the husband of Crescence Consigny, Charles's sister, was Rolland Pleau, a cousin of Ézilda's on her mother's side. We can suppose that this family tie led to Charles and Ézilda meeting. In all likelihood, Charles already knew Ézilda before leaving for Europe in 1914, since he wrote to her during the war and married her as soon as he got back in 1919. In those days, soldiers had no leave during their service, so Charles must have known Ézilda before going to war. original page

undatedHome

Daily life at 2179 Létourneux

Three small rooms, no hot water or bathtub, a wood stove in the kitchen, a shed at the bottom of the yard.

Nicole's text
Ézilda lived more than forty years at 2179 Létourneux. In fact, this flat had only three small rooms, plus a tiny toilet room a few feet square. The three rooms were a kitchen, at the end of the hall, and a double parlour at the entrance, on the left. The front part served as a parlour by day and as the girls' bedroom at night; the other part was Charles and Ézilda's bedroom. There was not even a door on these two rooms, so short was the space. The parents' bedroom was so small that it was impossible to open the two bottom drawers of the dresser at the foot of the bed. It held the only wardrobe in the whole flat. Curtains (a light sheer one and a heavier velvet one) could give privacy between the two rooms, depending on need and the season. The boy, Arsène, slept on a folding bed in the kitchen. By day the bed was folded up and pushed against the wall, near the wringer washer. The bed and the washer were covered with cloth covers to hide them a little. original page
No hot water and no bathtub; people could go to the Public Bath to wash. Otherwise they washed "with a facecloth". Heat came from the wood stove in the kitchen. Electricity provided the lighting, which is why Ézilda used the term "my light bill" when talking about her electricity bill. The radio was the only entertainment from the outside world. There was a small one in the kitchen and a big radio set in the parlour. original page
A small storage space stood at the end of the back balcony, and a shed at the bottom of the yard. Firewood was stored there. When Charles and Ézilda moved in, the land east of rue Létourneux (behind the Marché Maisonneuve) was still wasteland. The Consignys and their neighbours grew big vegetable gardens there. Later, Canadian Steel Foundries bought the land and fenced it in, because trains ran on it to deliver goods to the plant. original page

Marie-Anna's friendship with Imelda Verreault

Both born in 1897, neighbours on rue Delinelle. Imelda's daughter Rachel will marry Rosaire, one of Marie-Anna's sons.

Nicole's text
All her life Marie-Anna has good friends; some even appear in the group photos at her children's weddings. Her friendship with one of them, Imelda Verreault, goes back to childhood. They were both born in 1897 and were neighbours, Marie-Anna living at 54 and Imelda at 63 rue Delinelle. original page
Imelda is the first to marry, to a Mr. Henri Philippe. In 1916 Marie-Anna sends them her best wishes for the new year and invites them to her wedding the following month. Mr. Philippe will be the accountant of Rosario's garage, Marie-Anna's husband. Imelda's daughter, Rachel, will marry one of Marie-Anna's sons, Rosaire. Later Imelda will live for many years at 7078 Des Écores, one of the flats that had once belonged to Marie-Anna. Imelda dies in 2002 at the age of 104. original page
