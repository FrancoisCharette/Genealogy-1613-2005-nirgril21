Marie-Anna Caron is born in Montréal on April 27, 1897. Her father, Arthur, is a tinsmith. He dies when she is eight years old. Her mother, Alphonsine Gagnon,  then supports the family by taking in other people's laundry. In 1913 she remarries a widower, Zénon Laframboise. Marie-Anna receives a school education. Although her parents come from the Bas du Fleuve (the lower St. Lawrence), the family spends its whole life in the Saint-Henri district. Ézilda Côté is born in Québec City, in the Saint-Sauveur district, on December 27, 1891. Her father, Gaudias, is a shoemaker. Ézilda has no schooling, but she can sign her name. The whole family moves to Montréal in 1903, to the new industrial town of Maisonneuve. They find work there and will spend the rest of their lives there. Ézilda works in a shoe factory until her marriage. 

Marie-Anna  marries Rosario Charette on January 16, 1917, in St-Zotique parish in Montréal. It is a simple wedding, because Rosario has been in mourning for his father since the previous summer. His mother having also passed away, the couple settles into Rosario's parents' flat, furniture and all, at 1121 St-Antoine, St-Henri. Two years later they move to 444 St-Émélie, close to Marie-Anna's mother. Ézilda  marries Charles Consigny on July 28, 1919, in Très-St-Nom-de-Jésus parish in Montréal. It is a simple wedding, as Ézilda is in mourning for her father. Charles has only been back home since the end of May; he had served in the war in Europe for nearly five years. The young couple lives in Maisonneuve, probably with Ézilda's mother, Georgianna, during the first years.

Marie-Anna has seven children, one of them stillborn.  She raises her family at 910 St-Philipppe, St-Henri, near the garage her husband had built, and runs, at 4271 St-Antoine. The family is comfortable; the children attend private schools. On weekends they visit relatives at their cottages, and they spend their holidays at the fashionable resorts of the day. They own a car, change it often, and travel, for example to Lac St-Jean to visit Marie-Anna's relatives.  Ézilda has five children, two of whom die in infancy. In 1925 the couple settles at 2179 Létourneux, Maisonneuve. It is a modest flat, where she raises her family with her husband, a day labourer. In 1940 Charles re-enlists in the army. As soon as they finish elementary school, the children go out to work. On fine summer Sundays, the family joins relatives and friends for picnics nearby, for example at the Botanical Garden, just around the corner.

Marie-Anna loses her husband on May 21, 1940. He dies suddenly, though his health had been poor in the last part of his life. She has to rebuild her life, not least her finances. She rents a flat on rue St-Gérard and goes on raising her family there. Her eldest daughter is married from there the following year; her youngest is at boarding school. The boys  start working or learn a trade. Two of her sons serve in the war in Europe.  Ézilda loses her husband on October 19, 1941. He dies suddenly in Kingston, where he is working for the Canadian army. For the rest of her life she receives a widow's pension, which gives her a minimum of financial security. She stays in the same flat; her children help her by paying "room and board" and with the hardest daily chores (for example, carrying the firewood up to the second floor, since she had a wood stove for cooking and heating).  

 
On the morning of June 29, 1946, the bells ring out in Saint-Jean-Baptiste-de-Lasalle parish on rue Hochelaga, at the corner of Pie IX.  Marie-Anna and Ézilda share one and the same event: the wedding of Gérard, Marie-Anna's son, and Jacqueline, Ézilda's daughter. They share the wedding meal at the newlyweds' head table. Had they met beforehand to get acquainted? No one knows anymore. The wedding was arranged quickly, Gérard having come back from Europe only a few weeks earlier. That same day Ézilda marries off both her daughters, and her son follows suit the next December. So Ézilda finds herself alone. As for Marie-Anna, she marries off her four sons between May 1945 and April 1948. She too ends up alone, her youngest, Ghislaine, being at boarding school. Even so, because of the housing shortage caused by the war, Marie-Anna and Ézilda each have to share their flat for a while with some of their newly married children.  

From 1946 to 1964 Marie-Anna has twenty-one  grandchildren. In the 1950s she lives on the ground floor of a six-unit building that she bought on rue des Écores, in Montréal. Two of her sons and their families live in flats in the same building.  Marie-Anna loves anything new and modern and buys it as soon as it comes on the market: a refrigerator, a shower, a TV... She always likes to have the latest model of everything, and her children buy it for her when they go to New York now and then.  From 1947 to 1964 Ézilda has sixteen grandchildren. She still lives on rue Létourneux. She devotedly helps her two daughters through their "relevailles" (the weeks of rest after childbirth) after each birth. Ézilda loves listening to the radio and often goes to the movies in the neighbourhood. She only buys herself a  refrigerator or a TV at the very end of her life. Modern gifts don't interest her; she puts them away, still in their wrapping, at the back of the closet. For example,  rather than an electric iron, she prefers  her old irons, which she heats on her wood stove. 

Marie-Anna dies on July 8, 1966, in Marieville. She had been living for some years in a nursing home run by nuns, needing constant care after a cancer of the intestines.  Ézilda dies on October 17, 1964, in her flat in Montréal, where she lived alone. She is said to have died of breathing problems after some painting work she had undertaken herself.
