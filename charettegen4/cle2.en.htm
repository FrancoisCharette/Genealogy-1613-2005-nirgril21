We saw the third Choret generation move to the Montréal area shortly before 1720; the following generations would go on living nearby.  
The fourth generation lived mainly in Sault-au-Récollet (Ahuntsic), but that did not stop them from buying land in the surrounding area as well as on the other side of the Rivière des Prairies.  

The genealogical data show that people settled near their relatives ("par apparenté").  Most of them lived, married and died in the same area.  
This would continue  until the seventh Choret generation, which moved farther north, to Saint-Jérôme.  

In the 17th century, the population settled mainly around the colony's three towns, Montréal, Québec and Trois-Rivières.  For years, Iroquois raids slowed population growth, but as soon as the "Great Peace" took hold, development resumed with a vengeance.  In 1709, Gédéon de Catalogne drew up maps showing that the inhabitants were concentrated mainly in the St. Lawrence valley, from Rivière-Ouelle to Les Cèdres, in the Montréal area.

The St. Lawrence was in fact the main transportation route, so the seigneuries were granted first along the river.  Not until the 18th century did settlement begin to spread inland.  The space between Montréal and Québec filled up more and more.  In the oldest seigneuries there were second and even third rangs (rows of farms).  When Pehr Kalm, a Finnish naturalist, visited New France in 1749, he described the landscape in these terms:

"The country we are passing is everywhere quite beautiful, and it is a pleasure to see how prettily and how densely it is settled on both banks of the river; one could almost say that it forms a continuous village beginning at Montréal and stretching to Québec, a distance of at least thirty Swedish miles; that is quite a long village!  Almost everywhere, in fact, the farms touch, standing about three or four arpents apart."

During the 18th century, many people left the towns for the countryside, so much so that toward the end of the French Regime, 15% of the population lived in town and 85% in the country.  Before settling, people made sure they had family or friends nearby, because mutual help was essential to survival.  So it was not unusual to find a cluster of 10 to 50 dwellings in one place; these were called côtes rather than villages.  This closeness was due as much to geography as to family ties, and among other things it let people stick together in hard times.

So people generally settled in the countryside near their relatives.  A father with a large enough farm, for example, would leave part of it to his children so they could build their homes there.  The authorities soon sought to control the subdivision of land so that some people would not end up with properties that were too small.  They even went so far as to tear down houses built on lots judged too small.

A farm covered about 90 arpents.  It had a house, a barn, a stable, and ploughland, more than a third of it given over to grain.  Another part, in meadow and fodder, fed the animals.  The rest of the land was in "bois debout" (standing timber), which provided firewood and building timber.  Generally the farmer  made sure to be self-sufficient and went to town only to buy what he could not make himself: tools, nails, pins, cloth, etc.

The population of the St. Lawrence valley grew very fast, from 23,000 people in 1720 to about 55,000 in 1755 and about 70,000 in 1760.  Natural increase was considerable, since of those 70,000 inhabitants only an estimated 9,000 to 10,000 were French immigrants, the rest having been born in the country.
