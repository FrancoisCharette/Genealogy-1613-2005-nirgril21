Several questions arise about Sébastienne Veillon... the first being her date of birth.

The documents do not agree; Sébastienne's birth is placed between 1616 and 1626.  But the most likely date is between 1616 and 1618.  So it was a woman of about thirty who married Mathieu Choret and sailed with him for New France in 1647.

Her life in this new country was one of hard toil, a woman working as hard as her husband to clear and settle their land.   Even when pregnant, the work went on.  Sébastienne gave birth to seven children, including twins.
NEIGHBOURS, FRIENDS AND RELATIVES

Sébastienne's social fabric was built above all on neighbours and friends. Some relatives may also have come to join her in the country.  Perhaps a cousin... a Judith Veillon spent some time in New France.  

Judith Veillon is the daughter of Pierre Veillon and Renée Girard; we do not know the link between Pierre Veillon and Maixent Veillon, Sébastienne's father.  What is certain is that they were contemporaries and lived in the same part of France.  Judith was born on January 3, 1613, in La Rochelle,  and married René Ménard on July 9, 1636, in the same place.  Two years after Sébastienne left,  in 1649, Judith gave birth in La Rochelle to a daughter, Barbe Ménard.   

Judith probably came to the country with her daughter.  What is certain is that Barbe married Antoine Vermet on August 26, 1669, on the Île d'Orléans, in the parish of Sainte-Famille.  They raised a family there and spent their whole lives there. 
"TIGHTLY KNIT"
This expression is often used for Quebecers who have several ancestral lines going back to the 17th century.  It could hardly be otherwise, since the population grew from a core of people who intermarried and had many descendants.  

When you look at the data from the 1666 and 1667 censuses, you find among Mathieu and Sébastienne's neighbours family names that match those of their sons' future wives.  There were often several marriages between two families.

Take, for example, the LOIGNONS.

Pierre Loignon came from the Perche region of France.  A bachelor, he was hired by Noël Juchereau on March 4, 1647, the very day Mathieu and Sébastienne were married in La Rochelle.  So he sailed for Canada at the same time as they did.  Perhaps they got to know each other crossing the Atlantic on the same ship.

Pierre Loignon married Françoise Roussin in 1652 in Quebec.  They lived on a farm not far from Mathieu's on the Côte Notre-Dame-des-Anges, according to the 1666 census.  When their first child was born, they asked Mathieu and Sébastienne to be godfather and godmother to their daughter.  The baptism took place on September 7, 1653, in Quebec.  The child took her godmother's name, as custom required.  So there was a Sébastienne Loignon.  

Pierre and Françoise had more than twelve children.  One of their daughters, Anne, would marry Joseph Choret, Mathieu's second son, in 1676.  Their youngest son, Charles-Joseph, would marry, in 1695, the daughter of Jeanne Choret, and so Mathieu's granddaughter.  Another Loignon daughter, Jeanne, would marry, in 1686, Jean Gagnon, son of the ancestor Robert of the Gagnon line.  Not to mention that Robert Choret would marry a Paradis who was the sister-in-law of Nicolas Roussin, Françoise's brother.   And so on and so on... it was a network of marriages among all these families of early settlers.

Sébastienne is widowed in March 1664.  

She has seven children in her care, aged two to fifteen.  The eldest son, Robert, is not old enough to act as head of the family.  The only solution for Sébastienne is to remarry quickly.

SÉBASTIENNE'S SECOND

   MARRIAGE,

             TO

   PIERRE AUFROY

On August 25, 1664, in the parish of Notre-Dame de Québec, Sébastienne marries Pierre Aufroy.  They had signed a marriage contract the same day before the notaire Paul Vachon.

The son of Pierre Aufroy and Hilaire Milsandre, Pierre was born around 1635 at Notre-Dame-de-Bon-Port in Les Sables-d'Olonne, in Poitou.  He emigrated with a young nephew, Nicolas Menanteau, born around 1649 in the same parish.  Uncle and nephew were confirmed together in Quebec on March 23, 1664.  Nicolas would also marry a widow and settle on the Île d'Orléans as a farmer.

Pierre had not been in the country long, since he was an engagé of the surgeon Jean Madry, and we have seen that indenture contracts lasted three years.  In the summer of 1664 Pierre was not a free man, as his contract had not ended.  So Madry opposed the publication of the banns for Pierre and Sébastienne's marriage.  The case was taken to the Sovereign Council.  The Council handed down a decision on August 16, 1664, in Pierre's favour, recognizing "the freedom he must have to settle in this country and to take a wife there".

WHY THIS MARRIAGE?

There was an age difference of nearly twenty years between Sébastienne and Pierre.  One may wonder what each of them gained from this marriage and whether they really lived together.  

For Sébastienne, she had to ensure the survival of her family.  And for that, a head of the family was essential.  At that time, a woman was considered a minor all her life, first under her father's guardianship, then her husband's.  It was not socially acceptable for Sébastienne to stay alone on her land with her young children, and still less for her to take on a hired man, which would have given her a bad reputation.  There had to be, officially at least, a head of the family.  In any case, staying alone was economically impossible, as farm work was too hard. 

We have already seen that romance had no place in the idea of marriage at that time, and that the couple, in a colony being settled, was the basis of the family enterprise.  Widowers and widows with young children all remarried quickly; "blended families" were common, and the second union often produced more children.  Only older widowers and widows could remain single.  So Sébastienne's only daughter, Jeanne, would not remarry after her husband's death, since she could count on her grown children to work the land and look after her.

For Pierre, this marriage freed him from his indenture and let him settle in the country.  Sébastienne had inherited half of Mathieu's property, which was no small thing.  In 1664 there were very few marriageable women in the colony; the population of about 3,000 people scattered along the St. Lawrence was mostly men.  The gradual arrival of the "Filles du Roy" (King's Daughters) had begun the year before, but they were not numerous and chose men who were already established, which Pierre Aufroy was not.

Doubts about whether Sébastienne and Pierre really lived together come from the many inconsistencies found in the documents:

(it goes without saying that these documents are not 100% reliable)

  • 1666 census  Côte Notre-Dame-des-Anges

Bastienne Veillon, 40, widow of Mathieu Choret;  Robert, 18;  Joseph, 14;  Jeanne, 13; Pierre and Ignace, 10;  Jean, 8; Pierre, 4;  Michel Pottier, 21, hired servant.

Where is Pierre Aufroy?  Sébastienne says she is 40 and the widow of Mathieu; she is supposedly living alone with her seven children and a hired man.  The following year she will say she is 50.

  • 1667 census  Côte Notre-Dame-des-Anges

Pierre Aufroy, 30;  Sébastienne Veillon, 50;  Pierre 13;  Joseph, 18;  Jeanne, 15;  Jean, 10;  Pierre, 6;  Ignace, 13;  5 head of livestock, 16 arpents cleared.

  • 1667 census  Île d'Orléans

Pierre Aufroy, 32;  tenant farmer of the Sieur de la Vigne, 12 arpents

In 1667, Pierre is listed in two different places, and his age differs.  

Robert Choret,  Sébastienne's eldest son, has left the family.  We find him alone on a 2-arpent farm on the Île d'Orléans, near Antoine Dionne and Robert Gagnon, two ancestors of other lines.  Pierre Loignon has also now settled with his family on the island, on a large farm of 50 arpents.

  • 1681 census  Petite Auvergne (in fact it is still the same farm in Beauport).

Sébastienne Veillon (widow of Pierre Aufroy) 65;  children: Pierre and Ignace (twins) 25, Jean 23, Pierre 19;  2 guns, 8 head of cattle;  20 arpents cleared.

  • 1681 census  County of St-Laurent ( Île d'Orléans)

Pierre Auffroy, 46

Nicolas Menanteau 32; his wife Jeanne Jousselot, 32; children: Michel 9, Pierre 3; 3 arpents of cleared land.

In 1681 Sébastienne is alone, still on Mathieu's land, with four of her sons who are still unmarried, and she declares herself the widow of Pierre Aufroy.  The land had continued to be cleared, since 4 arpents had been added.

In the same census Pierre Aufroy is listed as being 46 and living on the Île d'Orléans with his nephew and his family.   Pierre's name appears alone on a line, with no mention of family or property.  Then comes his nephew's, so they lived together.

  • Hôtel-Dieu archives.

On April 25, 1690, Sébastienne spends some time at the Hôtel-Dieu of Quebec, and in its archives she is described as "widow first of Mathieu Choret and secondly of Pierre Aufroy".  

Yet Pierre Aufroy died on the Île d'Orléans on November 17, 1700.  

He was buried on November 18, 1700, at St-François, Île d'Orléans.

It seems the island was Pierre's main residence, first as tenant farmer for the Sieur de la Vigne, then living with his nephew, the very one with whom he had emigrated, Nicolas Menanteau.  The latter would die at the Hôtel-Dieu on July 2, 1710.

Why does Sébastienne declare herself a widow when she is not?   

It's anybody's guess.  Send us your hypotheses.  But the idea that there were two Pierre Aufroys at the same time seems ruled out, since there is no mention of it in the documents.  It is certain there was only one, even if the spelling of his name sometimes varies (Offroy, Auffroy), which was quite common at the time.

DEATH OF SÉBASTIENNE VEILLON

Sébastienne dies in Beauport on December 21, 1698.  So she had spent the rest of her life in New France, more than fifty years, on the land granted by the Jesuits to Mathieu Choret.  

The records say she is 80 and that she "died in childhood" (morte en enfance).  Today we would say she had "fallen back into childhood", that is, into senility.   This condition means that her family must have looked after her during the last part of her life..  

Four of her children settled on the Île d'Orléans; the other three lived in Beauport: the two twins, Pierre and Ignace, and Pierre the Younger.  One of them   took over the family farm, and Sébastienne very probably lived with him and his family. 

 What is certain is that in 1709, when Gédéon de Catalogne drew up the map of the Quebec area, at the government's request, Mathieu's ancestral land still belonged to a Choret.
