CAP ST-IGNACE

Cap-Saint-Ignace is one of the oldest parishes on the Côte-du-Sud.  Every spring and autumn thousands of wild geese stop over here.  

Joseph Caron (2nd generation), born in 1652 in Quebec City, settles in the Bas du Fleuve. 

In 1678 Joseph  begins clearing land at Port-Joly. He lives with his brother Pierre at Cap-Saint-Ignace. He is an excellent carpenter and builds several houses and barns for the settlers.

In March 1683 Joseph Caron helps build  the first church of Cap-Saint-Ignace. It is made of wood and measures 40 feet long by 25 feet wide. This first church of Cap-Saint-Ignace stood in the Seigneury of Gamache, near the river. It was flooded but, even today, at low tide, you can see the stones marking where the church stood.

On November 23, 1684, Joseph marries Élisabeth Bernier at Cap-Saint-Ignace.  The  Berniers are one of the founding families  of the place. He dies there in 1711,  leaving five children.


The present church of Cap-Saint-Ignace obviously has nothing to do with the wooden one built by our ancestor in 1683


Plaque in front of the church telling its eventful history.   It mentions that Bishop de St-Vallier had found the wooden church built by Joseph Caron "rather pretty"


A very fine view of the inside of the church of Cap-Saint-Ignace


The choir of the church
Where does the name Cap-Saint-Ignace come from?  The name first referred to a small cape forming a peninsula halfway between Montmagny and L'Islet, opposite the present parish church.  As for the name Saint-Ignace, there is nothing definite about it in the archives.  The name was probably given by the Jesuits, in honour of their founder.  In their book, the Jesuit Relations, we see that they often passed through this place when accompanying the Indigenous people on their hunting trips.

The present church dates from 1891.  It was built between May  and October to plans by David Ouellet, a Quebec City architect.  The outside is of rusticated Deschambault stone;  the church is 165 feet long by 64 wide and 40 high.  The steeple is 210 feet high, cross included.  The two-storey sacristy is 50 feet long by 36 wide and ends in a chapel 12 feet in diameter.   

Inside, the paintings of the Stations of the Cross were painted by an artist from Lévis, Joseph Scherrer.  He also painted the four pictures in the choir recalling various events in the life of Saint Ignatius.

The present organ dates from 1918 and is the work of the Casavant firm of St-Hyacinthe; it bears the number 757.  It has three manuals, thirty-seven speaking stops and a 32-foot pipe, not to mention a solid oak case, making it a unique and remarkable instrument in the region.

The bells come from the Mears and Steinback foundry in England.  The big bell is named Marie Alice, the middle one Marie Zoé and the smallest Marie Joséphine.  Not counting the little sacristy bell.

The chandeliers are by Eugène Belleau, the parish tinsmith.

(This information comes from the parish bulletin of Cap-Saint-Ignace.)
