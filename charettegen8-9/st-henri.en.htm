SAINT-HENRI-DES-TANNERIES

A NEIGHBOURHOOD BURSTING WITH LIFE

Rue Saint-Antoine and its surroundings. Five generations of Charettes lived here for seventy years. Between 1870 and 1940, three were born here and four died here. 

Halfway between Lachine and Montréal, Saint-Henri-des-Tanneries owes its name to the fur merchants of New France, who opened tanneries there to prepare the hides they received from the trappers of the Pays-d'en-Haut. 

In 1840, Upper and Lower Canada are merged into a United Canada with an English-speaking majority. The world is changing in this society in the midst of intense demographic mixing, above all with the mass arrival of the Irish. Steam speeds up shipping and, through the railway, the links with the interior of the country. In 1867  Canadian Confederation gives new momentum to the building of Canada.

The Lachine Canal, dug by hand and opened in 1825, allows a large and very diverse manufacturing centre to flourish along its banks, right up until 1945. Working-class districts grow up around it, Saint-Henri among them. Workers' lives are very hard: starvation wages for a 6-day, 60-hour week, frequent workplace accidents and unemployment in winter, with no compensation at all. A quarter of the workers are under sixteen, some start at ten, and a third are women. After many workers' riots, the Canadian government, at the end of the 19th century, imposes better sanitary conditions at work and allows workers the right to organize. 

In wooden houses that are often unhealthy, where epidemics of typhus, cholera and smallpox are common, the workers, despite their poverty, never forget to get together with family and neighbours. They throw parties, play sports, sing in the choir. But it is the church that is the heart of social life, with Sunday mass and the religious feasts. 

The Catholic Church becomes a major player in society. From the middle of the 19th century, the clergy takes control of French-Canadian society and meddles in every aspect of its life, even, and above all, its morals. It will impose its dictates and its censorship, and hold, for a century, a monopoly over associations, the press, hospitals,  schools and universities. Little by little it becomes the voice of French-Canadian nationalism, and it pins the people to an agricultural calling, a utopia, given the poor state of farming in Québec. The motto seems to be: "nothing must change". The model is "Maria Chapdelaine".
Many farmers leave their land for the city, where factories are opening in which the whole family can work without training, most of them being illiterate. Others choose the United States: in 1900 the population of Québec origin there is estimated at 1,200,000, while Québec itself has 1,600,000. To stem this emigration, the clergy has new settlement lands opened up, such as the Témiscamingue.  

It will take more than a century, and courage, for Québec society to free itself from the stifling straitjacket of the Catholic Church, from its "ultramontane" nationalism, which holds that Québec must be led by the Church in order to assert itself, and from those myths, now turned into caricatures, of the "good French-speaking Catholic, working the land and raising his large family in poverty".

THE TWO ALPHONSES IN SAINT-HENRI

Alphonse, father and son, are masonry contractors; their houses are of stone and their lives easier than those of the factory workers. But they live fully in the heart of Saint-Henri and share in its teeming life. Epidemics strike the children of the poor and of the better-off alike, as we saw with the family of Alphonse Jr. 

They are good citizens but, above all, good Catholics, because in those days "outside the Church, there is no salvation". Especially for a contractor who needs contracts to make a living. The clergy, at the height of its power, has many churches, convents, etc. built... all in fine grey cut stone. And that is the work of Alphonse father and son. So they were certainly on the best of terms with the clergy, probably out of religious conviction, since that was the only path taught and socially tolerated, but also out of business interest.
