THE WAR TO END ALL WARS 

Those who lived through it gave it this name, la der des ders, the last of the last. To hold out in that hell, to find meaning in it and the will to go on, all those soldiers ended up waging war on war. So that it would be the last... Then it became the Great War; now we call it the First World War.

Charles Consigny, my maternal grandfather, fought in that war, and came back alive! That is surprising when you see even the smallest piece of information about this war, called a butchery, a dirty war, which killed ten million people and left more than twice as many physically or psychologically disabled, the gueules cassées (the disfigured), many of whom had to be institutionalized for life. 

Charles does not lie in a cemetery at Ypres, Arras, Vimy or the Somme. Luckily for us, he came home and had descendants. But how could he survive that hell, bear the suffering and the horror, the mud and the vermin, the fear and the anguish? And those vain and stupid offensives that mowed men down by the thousands?

By combining the war diaries (1), Charles's service file supplied by the Archives of Canada (2), various websites, documentaries, books, etc., I will try to follow Charles from August 13, 1914, to May 26, 1919, and so get a glimpse of his life as a "soldier for King and Empire".
(1)  The war diaries for each military unit and each medical unit are available on the federal Veterans Affairs website. It is the custom in every war for a non-commissioned officer to be appointed to keep the daily diary of his unit, which is then passed on to the Commanding Officer and then to the archives. It contains no personal information, only the day's activities, the weather, the number of men, etc... This diary is handwritten in English in a small notebook. Canada was a Dominion of the British Empire.

(2)  Charles Consigny's service file, obtained from the Archives of Canada, includes his medical file, his military postings and his banking details, that is, his pay deposits and withdrawals. It makes no mention of the battles he took part in, or other information of that kind. To find that out, you have to cross-check all the data.

N.B. To put things in context, note that when we speak of "salvoes" (rounds), it means that each gun in the unit fires one shot. So if there are ten guns, a salvo means ten shots fired together. Imagine the noise on days when hundreds, even thousands, of salvoes were fired.

Also, when it says that "the unit moves to...", remember that not only the men but also the guns, drawn by horses, the wagons, the ammunition, etc... moved too. Each unit was a self-contained whole that was moved like a pawn. These moves were mostly made on foot and at night.
